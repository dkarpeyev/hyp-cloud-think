# Formalizing quantum measurement: two directions

The review is in three parts:
- **Part A**, top-down: measurement in the standard Hilbert-space formalism, and the theorems that hold it together.
- **Part B**, bottom-up: axiom systems that rebuild the Hilbert-space picture, arranged by how primitive their starting notions are. Each comes with the conditions under which Hilbert space actually emerges.
- **Part C**: how far intro/elim (proof-theoretic) formalizations get, and a sketch of what a full one would need.

Citations are from memory and none of these papers are in THINK, so treat them as pointers to check.

---

## Part A — Measurement in the Hilbert-space formalism

### A1. Von Neumann/Lüders (sharp measurements)
- **Observable:** a self-adjoint operator A. By the **spectral theorem**, it has a projection-valued measure (PVM) E_A on the real line, and for a discrete spectrum A = Σ aᵢ Pᵢ.
- **Born rule:** p(aᵢ | ρ) = Tr(ρ Pᵢ).
- **State update (Lüders 1951):** ρ ↦ Pᵢ ρ Pᵢ / Tr(ρPᵢ). Von Neumann (1932) had a different rule for degenerate spectra. Lüders' rule is the *minimal-disturbance, repeatable* one.
- **Measurements of the first kind / ideal measurements:** measuring the same thing again immediately gives the same result, and observables compatible with A are not disturbed.

### A2. Generalized measurements
- **POVM** {Eᵢ}: effects 0 ≤ Eᵢ ≤ 1 with Σ Eᵢ = 1. These are needed for unsharp, noisy and informationally complete measurements. A SIC-POVM is the QBist "urgleichung" basis.
- **Instruments (Davies & Lewis 1970):** a map i ↦ ℐᵢ to completely positive maps such that Σᵢ ℐᵢ preserves trace. An instrument packages outcome probabilities Tr ℐᵢ(ρ) together with the conditional updates.
- **Kraus form:** ℐᵢ(ρ) = Σₖ Kᵢₖ ρ Kᵢₖ†. Lüders' rule is the special case Kᵢ = √Eᵢ.

### A3. Theorems that make this consistent
| Theorem | Content | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Naimark dilation** | every POVM is a PVM on a larger space | unsharp measurement = sharp measurement + ancilla |
| **Stinespring / Ozawa (1984)** | every CP instrument is realized by a *measuring process*: unitary coupling to a probe, then a PVM on the probe | the von Neumann chain is internally consistent, and the Heisenberg cut can be moved |
| **Gleason (1957)** | for dim ≥ 3, every additive probability measure on the projections has the form Tr(ρP) | the Born rule follows from the lattice of projections plus noncontextual additivity |
| **Busch (2003); Caves–Fuchs–Manne–Renes (2004)** | the same for effects/POVMs, including dim 2 | the "Gleason for POVMs" that QBism relies on |
| **Wigner / Uhlhorn** | symmetries that preserve transition probabilities (or orthogonality) are unitary or antiunitary | dynamics follows from the structure of measurement |
| **Kochen–Specker (1967)** | no noncontextual assignment of values to all projections when dim ≥ 3 | outcomes cannot be pre-existing values |

### A4. The measurement problem, in this language
Ozawa's theorem lets you describe the apparatus unitarily, but the *final* step is always a PVM on a pointer, applied using the Born and Lüders rules. Unitary evolution plus the Born/Lüders postulate is consistent. What it lacks is an account of *when* the postulate applies.

All the reconstructions in Part B start from **measurement as a primitive**, so they do not solve this problem. They show that **the Hilbert-space machinery is the unique representation of a certain logic or algebra of measurement**. That fits your MEAS/QBism thread: measurement is taken as basic and Hilbert space is derived from it.

## Part B — From axioms to Hilbert space

Ordered roughly from closest to physical measurement to most abstract.

### B1. Schwinger's measurement algebra (1959), the most literal "measurement first" approach
- **Primitives:** *measurement symbols* M(a), meaning "select the systems with outcome a".
- **Rules:**
  - M(a)M(a′) = δ_{aa′} M(a): repeatability and exclusivity.
  - Σₐ M(a) = 1: completeness.
- **Transition symbols** M(a, b): "accept b, emit a". They compose as M(a,b)M(c,d) = δ_{bc} M(a,d).
- **Measurements of incompatible quantities** are linked by *transformation functions* ⟨a|b⟩. Requiring the composition rules to hold across different complete measurements forces ⟨a|b⟩ to behave like inner-product amplitudes. Hilbert space then appears as a representation of the algebra.
- **What gets assumed:** that the transformation functions are complex numbers. Schwinger postulates this; he does not derive it.
- **Modern version:** Ciaglia, Ibort & Marmo, "Schwinger's picture of quantum mechanics" (2019–). The transitions form a **groupoid**, and the Hilbert space comes from the **GNS representation** of a state on the groupoid algebra.

### B2. Quantum logic: Birkhoff–von Neumann → Piron → Solèr
- **Primitives:** **yes/no tests**, or "properties". Piron's *questions* are defined operationally (the Geneva school: Piron, Aerts). Tests are ordered by "whenever α is certain, β is certain". Tests come with an orthocomplement.
- **Axioms and what they correspond to physically:**

| Axiom on the lattice L | Physical meaning |
|---|---|
| complete orthocomplemented lattice | tests can be combined and negated |
| **orthomodularity** | compatible tests behave classically; this is what ideal measurements require |
| **atomic** | pure states exist, as maximal information |
| **covering law** | an ideal measurement takes a pure state to a pure state |
| **irreducible** | no superselection rules |

- **Piron's representation theorem (1964):** if rank ≥ 4, L is isomorphic to the lattice of closed subspaces of a *generalized* Hilbert space over a **division ring K with involution**.
- **Solèr (1995):** if there is also an infinite orthonormal sequence, then K = ℝ, ℂ or ℍ, and the space is a real Hilbert space in the usual sense.
- **The measurement link.** **Pool (1968)** used Baer *-semigroups of "operations" to show that the covering law and orthomodularity match the existence of *ideal, first-kind* measurements. The **Sasaki projection** φₐ(b) = a ∧ (a′ ∨ b) is the lattice version of Lüders' rule.
- **What remains:**
  - Gleason's theorem then supplies the Born rule.
  - Choosing ℂ over ℝ or ℍ needs extra input: composite systems, or a continuous symmetry group (Stückelberg's argument for ℂ).
  - Rank 3 has exceptions, the non-Desarguesian planes.
  - Mackey's programme (1963) is the probabilistic version of this route. The covering law was its sticking point.

### B3. Operational and convex frameworks (GPTs)
- **Primitives:** preparations, transformations and effects. The states form a convex set, the effects are the dual cone, and probabilities come from pairing a state with an effect.
- **Background:** Ludwig (*Foundations of QM*, 1983) and Mielnik. **Effect algebras** (Foulis–Bennett 1994) abstract the POVM elements.
- **Finite-dimensional reconstructions:**

| Reconstruction | Main axioms | Axiom that specifically selects QM |
|---|---|---|
| **Hardy (2001)** | simplicity, subspaces, composition (K = K_A·K_B), **continuous reversibility** | continuity of pure-state transformations, which excludes classical theory |
| **Dakić–Brukner (2009)**; **Masanes–Müller (2011)** | the information unit is a *bit*; local tomography; continuous reversibility; all effects measurable | the state space of a single bit is a 3-ball, and dimension 3 is forced |
| **Chiribella–D'Ariano–Perinotti (2011)** | causality, perfect distinguishability, ideal compression, local distinguishability, pure conditioning, plus **purification** | every mixed state is the marginal of a pure state of a larger system, unique up to reversible transformation on the purifying system. In effect this is "the church of the larger Hilbert space" as an axiom |
| **Barnum–Müller–Ududec (2014)** | single-system: **ideal measurements** (repeatable, minimally disturbing), symmetry of the state space, **no third-order interference** (Sorkin) | the result is a Jordan algebra; this is the route most focused on measurement |
| **Höhn (2017); Höhn & Wever (2017)** | rules for how an **observer acquires information** by asking binary questions: limited information, complementarity, preservation | directly agent-centric, which is relevant to IDEA 1. It reconstructs qubit and n-qubit QM |

- **The Jordan route** explains *why* Hilbert space appears:
  1. The state cone is **self-dual and homogeneous**. These properties come from axioms about ideal measurements and filtering.
  2. **Koecher–Vinberg:** such a cone is the positive cone of a **Euclidean Jordan algebra**.
  3. **Jordan–von Neumann–Wigner (1934):** the simple ones are self-adjoint matrices over ℝ, ℂ or ℍ, spin factors, and the exceptional 3×3 octonionic algebra.
  4. **Composition kills the exotic cases.** Barnum, Graydon & Wilce (2020) show that requiring well-behaved composites leaves only real, complex and quaternionic QM. **Local tomography** (the state of a composite is fixed by local measurements) then picks ℂ.
- **An experiment on the ℂ condition:** Renou et al. (*Nature*, 2021) and follow-up experiments refute real-number QM in network Bell scenarios. This uses the standard (tensor-product) composition, so the choice of ℂ is tied to how systems compose.
- **Sequential products, the most measurement-focused algebraic axioms:**
  - Gudder & Greechie (2002) axiomatize a product a∘b on effects, read as "a, then b", with the intended model a∘b = √a b √a, which is Lüders' rule.
  - **van de Wetering (2019), "Sequential product spaces are Jordan algebras":** under natural conditions (finite dimension, continuity), a space with such a product is a Euclidean Jordan algebra. That links *the algebra of successive measurements* directly to the Jordan → Hilbert route above.

### B4. Categorical and process-theoretic routes
- **Dagger-compact categories** (Abramsky & Coecke 2004) give the general framework for processes.
- **Classical structures:** Coecke, Pavlović & Vicary (2013) show that in FdHilb, dagger-special commutative Frobenius algebras correspond exactly to **orthonormal bases**.
  - A measurement in a basis is then **the operation that copies and deletes classical data**. Measurement is defined by what it lets you copy.
  - This is the categorical counterpart of the `!` modality from linear logic.
- **Causality postulate** (Coecke & Kissinger): discarding a system is unique. Consequences: no signalling, and normalized measurement.
- **Selby, Scandolo & Coecke (2021), "Reconstructing quantum theory from diagrammatic postulates":** a reconstruction stated purely in terms of diagrams.
- **Heunen & Kornell (PNAS 2022), "Axioms for the category of Hilbert spaces":**
  - Axioms: a dagger symmetric monoidal category with dagger biproducts and dagger equalizers, a simple monoidal unit that generates, and directed colimits of dagger monomorphisms.
  - Result: such a category is equivalent to the category of real or complex Hilbert spaces.
  - This is the cleanest "Hilbert space emerges from structural rules" theorem, and the scalars ℝ/ℂ come out of it rather than being assumed.
- **Effectus theory** (Jacobs; Cho, Jacobs, B. & A. Westerbaan 2015): a categorical logic of states, predicates and their duality.
  - It has **comprehension** (restrict to where predicate p holds) and **quotient** (divide out where p fails), each defined by a universal property, i.e. an adjunction.
  - In quantum models, Lüders' operation `asrt_p` (ρ ↦ √p ρ √p) is built from these, and the construction picks out von Neumann algebras.
  - I recall the details only roughly; check the "quotient–comprehension chains" paper and the Westerbaans' and van de Wetering's theses.
  - This matters here because **adjunctions are the categorical form of intro/elim pairs** (Lawvere). Effectus theory is therefore the place where *measurement update = a composite of adjoint operations* is already a theorem.

### B5. Probability-calculus derivations
- **Goyal, Knuth & Skilling (PRA 2010):**
  - Primitives: **sequences of measurements**, combined *in series* (one after another) and *in parallel* (coarse-graining the outcomes).
  - Requiring associativity and distributivity of these combinations, together with a pair-valued representation, gives **complex amplitudes and Feynman's rules** (sum over paths, product along a path). The Born rule follows.
  - This is the closest to "consistency rules on combining measurements → complex Hilbert space", the way Cox derived probability theory.
- **QBism (Fuchs & Schack):** the Born rule, rewritten with SIC-POVMs, is a modified law of total probability (the "urgleichung"). The programme of deriving the full state space from this plus extra conditions is **unfinished**. The SIC existence problem (Zauner's conjecture) is still open in general.

### B6. Summary: under what conditions does Hilbert space emerge?

```
tests / measurements (primitive)
   │  + ideal (repeatable, minimally disturbing) measurements  ── Pool, BMU, sequential products
   ▼
orthomodular structure / self-dual homogeneous cone / sequential product
   │  + atoms (pure states) + covering law (ideal measurement keeps pure states pure)
   │  + irreducibility (no superselection)
   ▼
Jordan algebra  /  generalized Hilbert space over a *-division ring  ── Koecher–Vinberg, JvNW; Piron
   │  + rank ≥ 4 (or dimension/continuity conditions)
   │  + infinite orthonormal sequence  ── Solèr  ⇒  K ∈ {ℝ, ℂ, ℍ}
   │  + well-behaved composites  ── Barnum–Graydon–Wilce  ⇒ no exceptional or spin-factor cases
   ▼
real / complex / quaternionic Hilbert space
   │  + local tomography (or a continuous symmetry group, or Goyal–Knuth–Skilling pair-valued consistency)
   ▼
complex Hilbert space
   │  + Gleason / Busch (noncontextual probability on effects)
   ▼
Born rule;  Lüders rule = the instrument behind ideal measurement;  Ozawa: realizable unitarily
```

Each condition can fail:
- Dropping **ideal measurement** gives general GPTs, such as boxworld and PR boxes.
- Dropping **irreducibility** gives direct sums, i.e. superselection, and in general von Neumann algebras.
- Dropping **local tomography** leaves real QM.
- Dropping **noncontextual additivity** leaves the Born rule underdetermined.

### C2. What is missing
No current system:
1. states intro/elim rules for *tests* or *measurements* as connectives or modalities, and
2. proves a **representation theorem**, analogous to Piron or Heunen–Kornell, that every model satisfying the rules (plus listed conditions) is Hilbert-space QM.

The pieces exist separately:
- **Proof theory** gives linearity and unitarity (Díaz-Caro et al.).
- **Category theory** gives Hilbert space from structural rules (Heunen–Kornell) and measurement update from adjunctions (effectus theory).
- **Operational reconstructions** say which physical conditions are needed (Part B6).

### C3. Sketch of a natural-deduction system for measurement
This is a proposal of mine, not established work. The idea is to turn each condition in B6 into a rule.

- **Judgments:** `Γ ⊢ ρ : A` means "preparation ρ of system type A". `Γ ⊢ e : A ⊸ ⊥` means "effect/test on A".
- **Measurement as a modality.**
  - Intro: a test with outcome set X on A gives `meas_X : A ⊸ ⊕_{x∈X} (!x ⊗ A_x)`. Each outcome is classical and copyable, and the post-measurement system has type A_x.
  - Elim: case analysis on the outcome, in the style of ⊙ / ∨.
- **Rules as equations (β/η conversions):**
  - *Repeatability*, i.e. idempotence: `meas ; meas ≡ meas ; (copy outcome)`. This is the Schwinger/Lüders M(a)² = M(a), and corresponds to the Frobenius "copy" law.
  - *Minimal disturbance*, i.e. compatible tests commute: this gives orthomodularity.
  - *Purity preservation*: if a pure state is measured, every post-measurement state is pure. This is the covering law.
  - *Composition*: ⊗ obeys local tomography, so a test on A ⊗ B is determined by product tests.
  - *Coherence*: probabilities attached to eliminations satisfy Dutch-book coherence and noncontextual additivity. This is where your IDEA 1 reading of the Born rule as a *harmony condition* fits.
- **The target theorem** (which would have to be proved): every model of the system satisfying these equations is a sub-theory of the instruments on complex Hilbert spaces, and the Lüders rule is the normal form of `meas`.
- **Proof strategy:** build the **term model**, then show that its tests form an orthomodular lattice with covering law, or a sequential product space. Then apply Piron–Solèr or van de Wetering + JvNW, and use composition to fix ℂ.

The **Heunen–Kornell** route may be the easier one: show that the term category satisfies their axioms (dagger, biproducts, equalizers, a simple generating unit). This is the step nobody has done yet.

## Acronyms

## 1. The two definitions

Below, ρ is the state (a density operator) on a d-dimensional Hilbert space, and i labels the outcomes. In both cases the probability of outcome i is given by the **Born rule**, p(i) = Tr(ρ Eᵢ).

**Sharp measurement (PVM)** {Pᵢ}:
- each Pᵢ is a **projection**: Pᵢ² = Pᵢ, so its eigenvalues are only **0 or 1**;
- the projections are mutually **orthogonal**: PᵢPⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j;
- they sum to the identity: Σ Pᵢ = 𝟙;
- consequently there are **at most d** distinct outcomes.

**Unsharp, or generalized, measurement (POVM)** {Eᵢ}:
- each Eᵢ is an **effect**: a positive operator with 0 ≤ Eᵢ ≤ 𝟙, so its eigenvalues lie anywhere in **[0, 1]**;
- they sum to the identity: Σ Eᵢ = 𝟙;
- **no orthogonality** is required, and there can be **any number of outcomes**, including more than d.

A measurement is **sharp** if every effect is a projection, and **unsharp** otherwise.

---

## 2. Why POVM is the broader class, and why it can seem the other way round

**The containment.** Every projection is a positive operator with eigenvalues in {0, 1} ⊂ [0, 1]. So every PVM is a POVM, but not every POVM is a PVM. The word "projection" names the *special* case, and "positive operator" names the general one.

> Analogy: deterministic rules (outcome 0 or 1) are a special case of probabilistic rules (any number in [0, 1]). A projection is a yes/no test with no noise. An effect is a yes/no test that may have noise.

**Why it can feel reversed.** There are two good reasons.

1. **Each sharp measurement is better than any noisy version of it.** A sharp measurement extracts the most information about its observable, and an unsharp version extracts less. So in terms of *quality*, PVMs come first. But a larger class does not mean better members: POVMs include all the noisy, degraded measurements in addition to the ideal ones.
2. **Naimark's theorem: PVMs are more fundamental.** Every POVM on a system S *is* a PVM on a larger system, S plus an auxiliary system (an "ancilla" or probe). A POVM is how a sharp measurement of S + probe looks *when you describe only S*.
   - So: **on a fixed system**, POVMs ⊋ PVMs.
   - **Across all systems**, every POVM comes from some PVM.

   Both statements are true. Unsharpness is not new physics. It is the result of describing only part of the measured system.

**POVMs can also do things no PVM can on the same system:**
- have more than d outcomes. A qubit SIC-POVM has 4 outcomes, and its statistics alone determine the state completely, i.e. tomography with a single measurement setting;
- **jointly** measure incompatible observables, approximately (section 4);
- distinguish non-orthogonal states **unambiguously**, though only some of the time (section 5.6).

## 3. Sharp vs unsharp

| | Sharp (PVM) | Unsharp (POVM) |
|---|---|---|
| Eigenvalues of effects | 0 or 1 | in [0, 1] |
| Can some state give an outcome with **certainty**? | yes: eigenstates give p = 1 | not if all eigenvalues of Eᵢ are < 1; then no state makes outcome i certain |
| **Repeatable?** (same result if repeated immediately) | yes, with the Lüders update | in general **no**. With the update ρ ↦ √Eᵢ ρ √Eᵢ / p(i), applying √Eᵢ twice gives Eᵢ, not √Eᵢ, so the state keeps changing |
| Number of outcomes | ≤ d | any |
| Effects of different outcomes commute? | yes | not necessarily |
| Joint measurement of incompatible observables | impossible (σ_x and σ_z have no common PVM) | possible if each is made unsharp enough |
| Disturbance | maximal: collapses onto an eigenspace | can be made arbitrarily small (weak measurement) |
| Post-measurement state | essentially fixed by the Lüders rule | **not fixed by the POVM**: many instruments realize the same POVM |

On the last row: a POVM gives only the *probabilities*. The state after the measurement is given by an **instrument**, a family of CP maps ℐᵢ with Tr ℐᵢ(ρ) = Tr(ρEᵢ). Different physical devices can realize the same POVM yet leave the system in different states.

## 4. A worked example: a noisy spin measurement

Take a spin-½ particle (a qubit) and a spin detector along z with **sharpness** η, where 0 ≤ η ≤ 1:

  E₊ = ½(𝟙 + η σ_z),  E₋ = ½(𝟙 − η σ_z)

- **η = 1:** E± = |↑⟩⟨↑|, |↓⟩⟨↓|. This is the sharp Stern–Gerlach measurement, a PVM.
- **0 < η < 1:** the eigenvalues of E₊ are (1 ± η)/2. These are not projections. For the state |↑⟩, p(+) = (1 + η)/2 < 1, so even a z-eigenstate does not give a certain result.
- **η = 0:** E± = ½𝟙. A coin flip that carries no information.

**Physical origin.** This is exactly what you get from a sharp detector that **reports the wrong result with probability (1 − η)/2**, through miscalibration, stray fields, or electronics noise. Mixing sharp measurements with classical noise gives an unsharp one.

**Joint measurement (Busch 1986).** σ_x and σ_z cannot be measured together sharply. But noisy versions with sharpnesses η_x and η_z *can* be measured jointly by a single POVM if and only if

  η_x² + η_z² ≤ 1.

So you can measure both, at η = 1/√2 each. This is a precise form of complementarity: incompatibility does not forbid joint measurement, it puts a lower bound on how much noise the joint measurement needs.

## 5. Physical experiments

These are given in roughly increasing order of how specifically "quantum" the unsharpness is. References are from memory; please check them.

### 5.1 Finite-resolution position measurement (the most common case)
- A detector pixel or screen grain of width w does not project onto a point. Its effect is a **smeared** projection: E(x) = ∫ f(x − x′) |x′⟩⟨x′| dx′, where f is the resolution function.
- Examples: every camera, the spots of a Stern–Gerlach beam on a screen, the dots in a double-slit experiment. Real position measurements are always unsharp. A sharp position PVM is an idealization.

### 5.2 Inefficient photodetectors
- A detector with efficiency η clicks on an n-photon state with probability 1 − (1 − η)ⁿ. The "click" effect is E_click = 𝟙 − (1 − η)^n̂, where n̂ is the photon-number operator.
- This operator is not a projection. Single-photon avalanche diodes and superconducting nanowire detectors are described this way in quantum optics.

### 5.3 Joint position–momentum measurement: heterodyne detection
- **Arthurs & Kelly (1965)** described a model that measures *both* x and p at once, each unsharp.
- **In optics** this is **heterodyne detection**, or "eight-port homodyne" detection. The light is split, and the two quadratures (the optical analogues of x and p) are measured simultaneously.
- Its POVM is the **coherent-state POVM** E(α) = |α⟩⟨α| / π. The outcome distribution is the Husimi **Q-function**. The extra noise beyond a sharp measurement is exactly the minimum that the joint-measurement trade-off above allows.
- This is standard in quantum optics and continuous-variable quantum communication.

### 5.4 Partial which-path information in the double slit
This links back to your MEAS thread.
- Attach a which-path marker that only *partially* records the path. The resulting path measurement is unsharp.
- **Englert's duality relation (1996):** D² + V² ≤ 1, where D is how well the path can be distinguished and V is the interference visibility. A sharp path measurement (D = 1) destroys the fringes (V = 0), and an unsharp one keeps partial fringes.
- **Experiment:** Dürr, Nonn & Rempe (*Nature*, 1998), with an atom interferometer whose internal atomic states act as the path markers.

### 5.5 Weak measurements and partial collapse
- **Weak measurement:** couple the system only weakly to a pointer, then read the pointer sharply. The effects on the system are close to multiples of 𝟙, so each run gives little information and disturbs the state very little. This is the Naimark picture in practice (sharp on the pointer, unsharp on the system).
- **Aharonov, Albert & Vaidman (1988)** introduced "weak values".
- **Kocsis et al. (*Science*, 2011)** reconstructed average photon "trajectories" in a double slit from weak momentum measurements.
- **Lundeen et al. (*Nature*, 2011)** measured a wavefunction "directly" using weak measurements.
- **Katz et al. (*Science*, 2006)** made a *partial* measurement of a superconducting qubit and then **undid** it (Katz et al., *PRL*, 2008). This shows that unsharp collapse is gradual and, in principle, reversible.
- **Continuous monitoring:** Murch et al. (*Nature*, 2013) tracked individual "quantum trajectories" of a superconducting qubit during a continuous weak measurement. A sharp measurement is the end point of a continuous sequence of unsharp ones.

### 5.6 Unambiguous state discrimination: a POVM beats every PVM
- **Task:** decide whether a photon is in |ψ₁⟩ or |ψ₂⟩, which are non-orthogonal, *without ever making an error*.
- **Ivanovic–Dieks–Peres (1987–88):** use a 3-outcome POVM on a qubit, with outcomes "it's ψ₁", "it's ψ₂" and "don't know". No PVM on a qubit can achieve the optimal success rate.
- **Experiments:** Huttner et al. (*PRA*, 1996) with photon polarization, realizing the POVM by coupling to an extra path degree of freedom, as Naimark's theorem says is possible. Also Clarke et al. (*PRA*, 2001).

### 5.7 SIC-POVMs, the measurements QBism relies on
- A qubit SIC has 4 effects, Eᵢ = ½|ψᵢ⟩⟨ψᵢ|, where the |ψᵢ⟩ form a regular tetrahedron on the Bloch sphere.
- These effects are rank-one but **not projections** (each has eigenvalue ½). So the measurement is unsharp, yet it is informationally complete.
- **Experiments:** Medendorp et al. (*PRA*, 2011) with photon polarization; Bent et al. (*PRX*, 2015) for higher-dimensional photonic systems.

## 6. Interpretation

- Sharp measurements are the **limiting case**: noiseless, repeatable, maximally disturbing.
- Unsharp measurements are what laboratories **actually perform**, and what any measurement looks like when you account only for the system and not the probe.
- Ludwig, Kraus, Davies and **Busch, Lahti & Mittelstaedt** (*The Quantum Theory of Measurement*, 1991/1996) argued that the POVM, not the PVM, should be the basic notion of observable.
- **Busch's version of Gleason's theorem** (2003, from our earlier list) works with effects and holds even in dimension 2.
- In the reconstructions we discussed, operational frameworks (GPTs, effect algebras) start from **effects**. Sharp measurements are then a structural *condition* ("ideal measurements exist"), and that condition is one of the axioms that forces the Hilbert-space structure.

A detailed treatment: Busch, Lahti, Pellonpää & Ylinen, *Quantum Measurement* (Springer, 2016), ch. 9–11 and 14–15.

# The smeared position observable, done rigorously

**Short answer:**
1. The formula I wrote, E(x) = ∫ f(x − x′) |x′⟩⟨x′| dx′, is a **density**, not the POVM itself. The POVM is E(Δ) = ∫_Δ E(x) dx. The formula itself is a spectral integral and is rigorous.
2. **No function f makes E a projection-valued measure.** Only the degenerate "f = δ_a" (a point mass) does, and that just gives back the sharp position PVM, shifted by a.
3. On Radon–Nikodym: the norm (Bochner) version fails, because B(H) lacks the Radon–Nikodym property. A **weak\*** version works exactly when ‖E(Δ)‖ ≤ C·|Δ|. The sharp PVM fails that condition, and that failure is one way of seeing *why* it can't be sharp and have a density at the same time.

**Abbreviations used below:** PVM = Projection-Valued Measure; POVM = Positive Operator-Valued Measure; RN = Radon–Nikodym; RNP = Radon–Nikodym Property; a.e. = almost everywhere; H = L²(ℝ), the Hilbert space; B(H) = bounded operators on H; T(H) = trace-class operators; L¹, L^∞ = integrable and essentially bounded functions; |Δ| = Lebesgue measure of the set Δ.

---

## 1. What the formula means

**The sharp object.** On H = L²(ℝ), the position PVM Q assigns to each Borel set Δ the operator "multiply by the indicator χ_Δ":

  (Q(Δ)ψ)(x′) = χ_Δ(x′) ψ(x′).

The expression |x′⟩⟨x′| dx′ is physicists' shorthand for **dQ(x′)**. The kets |x′⟩ are not vectors in H, and |x′⟩⟨x′| is not an operator on H (section 3).

**The density.** With that reading,

  E(x) = ∫ f(x − x′) dQ(x′) = f(x − Q̂),

where Q̂ is the position operator. This is an ordinary **spectral integral** (functional calculus), and it is simply the multiplication operator by the function x′ ↦ f(x − x′).
- If f is bounded, E(x) is a bounded positive operator with ‖E(x)‖ = ‖f‖_∞.
- If f ∈ L¹ but unbounded, E(x) is an unbounded multiplication operator.

**The POVM.** The POVM is obtained by integrating the density over outcomes:

  E(Δ) = ∫_Δ E(x) dx = g_Δ(Q̂),  where g_Δ(x′) = ∫_Δ f(x − x′) dx = μ_f(Δ − x′),

and μ_f is the probability measure with density f.

**Conditions on f for E to be a POVM:** f measurable, f ≥ 0, and ∫ f = 1. Then:
- 0 ≤ g_Δ ≤ 1, so 0 ≤ E(Δ) ≤ 𝟙;
- g_ℝ = 1, so E(ℝ) = 𝟙;
- σ-additivity holds in the strong operator topology, by monotone convergence.

**Physical meaning.** f(x − x′) is the detector's **response function**: the probability density of reading x when the particle is at x′. The outcome density is the sharp distribution blurred by the noise:

  p(x) = Tr(ρ E(x)) = (f ∗ |ψ|²)(x)  for ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|.

**The general notion: smearing.** The density is not essential. Any probability measure μ (the noise distribution) gives a POVM

  E(Δ) = ∫ μ(Δ − x′) dQ(x′),  written E = μ ∗ Q.

More generally still, any Markov kernel κ(x′, Δ) gives E(Δ) = ∫ κ(x′, Δ) dQ(x′). This is a **smeared, fuzzy, or classically post-processed** version of Q:
- Ali, Emch and Prugovečki called these "fuzzy observables".
- Busch, Lahti and Mittelstaedt use the convolution notation μ ∗ Q.
- A known structural theorem says that **commutative POVMs are exactly smearings of PVMs** (Ali; Holevo; Jenčová & Pulmannová, "How sharp are PV measures?", 2007, if I recall the title correctly).

So the answer to "what is it?" is: a PVM composed with classical noise.

---

## 2. When is it a projection?

There are three separate questions.

**(a) Is the density E(x) a projection?** This happens exactly when f takes only the values 0 and 1 a.e. For example, f = χ_[−½, ½] gives E(x) = χ_[x−½, x+½](Q̂), a projection for every x.

This is **not meaningful**, though. A density is not an effect: it can exceed 𝟙, and rescaling the coordinate x ↦ λx multiplies it by 1/λ. So whether the density is idempotent depends on the coordinates chosen. The question that matters is about E(Δ).

**(b) If μ has a density f ∈ L¹, then for every Δ with 0 < |Δ| < ∞, E(Δ) is not a projection.**

*Proof.*
1. E(Δ) = g_Δ(Q̂) is a projection exactly when g_Δ ∈ {0, 1} Lebesgue-a.e. (Q's spectral measure has the same null sets as Lebesgue measure.)
2. g_Δ is the convolution of the L^∞ function χ_Δ with the L¹ function f(−·), so it is **uniformly continuous**.
3. A continuous function on ℝ (which is connected) that takes only the values 0 and 1 is constant.
4. By Fubini, ∫ g_Δ(x′) dx′ = |Δ|·∫ f = |Δ|, which is finite and nonzero. A constant 0 would give integral 0, and a constant 1 would give an infinite integral. So g_Δ is not constant, and E(Δ) is not a projection. ∎

So any density smearing, however narrow, makes **every** finite-measure outcome set unsharp. There is no "partly sharp" case.

**(c) E = μ ∗ Q is a PVM if and only if μ = δ_a.**

*Proof sketch.*
1. A POVM is a PVM exactly when it is multiplicative: E(Δ ∩ Γ) = E(Δ)E(Γ).
2. Multiplicativity means κ(x′, Δ ∩ Γ) = κ(x′, Δ)·κ(x′, Γ) for a.e. x′. Check this first on a countable generating algebra of sets, so that the a.e. exceptions can be combined.
3. Then, for a.e. x′, the measure μ(· − x′) takes only the values 0 and 1.
4. A {0,1}-valued Borel probability measure on ℝ (a Polish space) is a point mass. So μ = δ_a, and E(Δ) = Q(Δ − a), i.e. sharp position shifted by a. ∎

In other words, "f = δ" is the only choice, and δ is a measure, not a function.

## 3. In what sense the integrals exist, and Radon–Nikodym

There are three integrals to consider.

**(i) E(x) = ∫ f(x − x′) dQ(x′).** This is a spectral integral. For bounded f it is a norm limit of integrals of simple functions. For unbounded f it is defined on its natural domain. No RN argument is needed.

**(ii) E(Δ) = ∫_Δ E(x) dx.**
- **Weakly:** ⟨φ, E(Δ)ψ⟩ = ∫_Δ ∫ f(x − x′) φ̄(x′)ψ(x′) dx′ dx. Fubini applies because φ̄ψ ∈ L¹ and f ∈ L¹.
- **In norm (Bochner integral):** if f is bounded and uniformly continuous, then ‖E(x) − E(y)‖ = ‖f(x − ·) − f(y − ·)‖_∞ → 0 as y → x, so x ↦ E(x) is norm-continuous. The Bochner integral then exists over **bounded** Δ.
- **Over all of ℝ:** ‖E(x)‖ = ‖f‖_∞ for every x, so ∫_ℝ ‖E(x)‖ dx = ∞. The normalization ∫_ℝ E(x) dx = 𝟙 holds only in the strong or weak sense, never as a Bochner integral.

**(iii) Does an abstract RN theorem guarantee the density?**

**The norm version (Diestel–Uhl) fails, for two reasons:**
1. B(H) **does not have the RNP**. It contains ℓ^∞ isometrically, as the diagonal operators, and ℓ^∞ fails the RNP. So a B(H)-valued measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure need not have a Bochner density.
2. The sharp Q does not even have **bounded variation**. Partition Δ into n pieces of positive measure: each Q(piece) has norm 1, so the sum of norms is n, which is unbounded.

**The weak\* version works.**
- B(H) = T(H)\* is a dual space, and T(H) is separable.
- The dual of the Bochner space L¹(ℝ; T(H)) is L^∞_{w\*}(ℝ; B(H)), the bounded **weak\*-measurable** functions. This is the Dinculeanu / Ionescu Tulcea lifting theorem; see Diestel & Uhl, *Vector Measures* (1977), ch. IV.
- Consequence: **a POVM has a bounded weak\*-measurable density D(x) with E(Δ) = ∫_Δ D(x) dx (weak\*) if and only if ‖E(Δ)‖ ≤ C·|Δ| for some constant C.**
- The smeared observable satisfies this with C = ‖f‖_∞. The sharp Q does not, because ‖Q(Δ)‖ = 1 for every |Δ| > 0.

**This gives a second proof that sharpness and densities are incompatible.**
1. Suppose a POVM has a bounded density. Then ‖E(Δ)‖ ≤ C|Δ| < 1 whenever |Δ| < 1/C.
2. A projection with norm less than 1 is 0, so every E(Δ) with |Δ| < 1/C that is a projection must vanish.
3. If E were a PVM, every set of finite measure could be cut into pieces of measure less than 1/C, all mapped to 0. By additivity E = 0, which contradicts E(ℝ) = 𝟙.

So **a bounded operator density forces unsharpness**. Smearing is what turns the formal |x′⟩⟨x′| into a genuine operator.

**What |x′⟩⟨x′| is for the sharp Q.** The density exists only in a generalized sense, as a form rather than an operator:
- **Rigged Hilbert space** (a Gelfand triple S ⊂ L² ⊂ S′, with S the Schwartz functions): |x′⟩ ∈ S′, and ⟨φ|x′⟩⟨x′|ψ⟩ = φ̄(x′)ψ(x′) for φ, ψ ∈ S. The Gelfand–Maurin nuclear spectral theorem guarantees such generalized eigenvectors.
- **General POVMs:**
  - Take a scalar control measure ν (for example ν(Δ) = Tr(ρ₀E(Δ)) with ρ₀ faithful).
  - The scalar RN derivatives of the matrix elements ⟨φ, E(·)ψ⟩ with respect to ν fit together, for ν-a.e. x, into **positive sesquilinear forms** D(x) on a dense subspace. These forms may be unbounded and need not be closable.
  - See Hytönen, Pellonpää & Ylinen, "Positive sesquilinear form measures and generalized eigenvalue expansions" (*J. Math. Anal. Appl.*, 2007), and Pellonpää's direct-integral description of POVMs. I'm citing these from memory, so check them.
- **In finite dimension** none of these subtleties arise. Finite-dimensional spaces have the RNP, so E(dx) = P(x) ν(dx) with ν = Tr E and Tr P(x) = 1 (Chiribella, D'Ariano & Schlingemann, 2010).

# Everett, "'Relative State' Formulation of Quantum Mechanics" (1957)

**Citation:** Hugh Everett III, *Reviews of Modern Physics* **29**, 454–462 (1957). In the same issue, John Wheeler, Everett's thesis advisor, published a companion piece, "Assessment of Everett's 'Relative State' Formulation of Quantum Theory" (pp. 463–465).

The paper is a heavily shortened version of Everett's Princeton PhD thesis. The full original, *The Theory of the Universal Wave Function* (written 1955–56), was published only in 1973, in DeWitt & Graham (eds.), *The Many-Worlds Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics*.

The paper isn't in THINK, so this summary is from memory. The overall argument is reliable; treat wording and section-level details as approximate.

---

## 1. The problem Everett addresses

**Von Neumann's two processes.** In von Neumann's formulation, a state changes in two different ways:
- **Process 1:** a discontinuous, probabilistic jump at measurement. The state ψ becomes an eigenstate φᵢ with probability |⟨φᵢ|ψ⟩|².
- **Process 2:** continuous, deterministic, unitary evolution under the Schrödinger equation.

**Why this is a problem.** Process 1 needs an observer **outside** the system. That makes the theory unusable for:
- a **closed system that contains its observers**: the Wigner's-friend situation, which Everett's thesis discusses before Wigner's 1961 paper;
- **the universe as a whole**. This mattered to Wheeler, who was working on quantum gravity and cosmology, where there is no outside observer.

**The options.** Everett lists the possible ways out:
- solipsism: only one observer exists;
- quantum mechanics fails for apparatus or observers;
- observers obey special laws;
- the wavefunction is not a complete description (hidden variables);
- **Process 2 holds universally.** This is the one he adopts.

## 2. The proposal: "pure wave mechanics"

- **There is only Process 2.** A single **universal wavefunction** evolves unitarily, and nothing ever collapses.
- **Observers are physical systems** inside that wavefunction, described by the same theory.
- **The aim** is to *derive*, rather than postulate, the conclusion that observers *experience* Process 1: definite outcomes, apparent collapse, and Born-rule statistics.

The rest of the paper argues that the bare formalism, applied to observers, already predicts what Process 1 was introduced to explain.

## 3. Relative states

The key concept is the **relative state**. Take a composite system S = S₁ + S₂ in a state ψ, and choose a basis {ξᵢ} for S₁. Then ψ can always be written as

  ψ = Σᵢ aᵢ ξᵢ ⊗ ψ_rel[ξᵢ],

where the **relative state** of S₂, given that S₁ is in ξᵢ, is

  ψ_rel[ξᵢ] = Nᵢ Σⱼ ⟨ξᵢ ηⱼ | ψ⟩ ηⱼ,

with {ηⱼ} any basis of S₂ and Nᵢ a normalization constant.

What follows from this:
- **A subsystem has no state of its own in general.** When S₁ and S₂ are correlated (in today's terms, entangled), S₂ has a definite state only *relative to* a specified state of S₁. In Everett's words, roughly: "there does not, in general, exist anything like a single state for one subsystem of a composite system."
- **Relativity of states is a basic feature.** States are always relative to other subsystems, much as motion in mechanics is always relative to a frame of reference.

## 4. The model of measurement

### 4a. The observer as a machine with memory
Everett deliberately models the observer as an automatically functioning machine, a **servomechanism**, which has:
- sensory apparatus that couples to the system;
- a **memory**, written as a list of recorded results ψ^O[A, B, C, …].

Experience is represented by the **memory configuration**. Nothing beyond the physics is needed.

### 4b. A "good observation"
A good observation of a quantity with eigenstates φᵢ is a unitary interaction that:
1. leaves eigenstates of the system unchanged, and
2. writes the eigenvalue into the observer's memory:

  φᵢ ⊗ ψ^O[…] ⟶ φᵢ ⊗ ψ^O[…, αᵢ].

This is a von Neumann **premeasurement**: system and apparatus become correlated through a unitary interaction. The difference is that the "apparatus" here is the observer.

### 4c. Superposition and branching
Because the evolution is linear, an initial superposition gives

  (Σᵢ aᵢ φᵢ) ⊗ ψ^O[…] ⟶ Σᵢ aᵢ φᵢ ⊗ ψ^O[…, αᵢ].

- **No single outcome is singled out.** The total state is still a superposition.
- **Each term contains an observer who saw one definite result** αᵢ, with the system in the matching eigenstate φᵢ.
- **Collapse is relative.** *Relative to* the observer state ψ^O[…, αᵢ], the system's state is φᵢ. So "collapse" is a fact about relative states, not a physical process.

Everett's image: the observer's memory over time is "not a linear path but a branching tree". In a note added in proof, he answered the objection that nobody *feels* the branching by comparing it with not feeling the Earth move under Copernican astronomy. He also stressed that all branches are equally actual.

### 4d. Why the observer sees the usual phenomena
Within each branch, the model reproduces the usual phenomenology:
- **Repeatability:** measuring the same system again gives memory [αᵢ, αᵢ] in every branch, so the system looks as if it collapsed.
- **Apparent randomness:** for incompatible measurements, or for many systems prepared identically, the memory sequences look random from inside a branch.
- **Agreement between observers:** if several observers measure the same system and compare notes, every branch shows consistent records. Communication is just another interaction that creates correlations.

### 4e. Probability: the measure on branches
To recover the Born rule, Everett puts a **measure** on the branches.
- **The requirement:** the measure should be **additive** in a way that stays consistent when branches split further (an amplitude aᵢ that splits into sub-branches must have a weight equal to the sum of their weights).
- **The result:** he argues this forces the measure to be m(aᵢ) = |aᵢ|².
- **The conclusion:** for long sequences of measurements, in **almost all** branches, measured by m, the relative frequencies in the memory match the Born rule. Process 1's statistics show up as what typical observers record.

## 5. Summary of the measurement model

| | Von Neumann / Copenhagen | Everett |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamics | Process 2 + Process 1 | Process 2 only |
| Observer | outside, classical | a physical system with memory, inside the wavefunction |
| Measurement | interaction + collapse | interaction only: premeasurement that correlates memory with system |
| Definite outcome | physical fact after collapse | fact *relative to* an observer's memory state |
| Born rule | postulate | claimed derivation from a measure on branches + typicality |
| Applies to the universe? | no (needs an outside) | yes |

In the terms of our earlier discussion, Everett's measurement is **Ozawa's measuring process without the final PVM**. The pointer, here the observer's memory, is never read by anything outside the system.

## 6. Reception and known problems

**History.**
- Wheeler's companion "assessment" softened the paper's tone, partly to accommodate Bohr.
- Everett's 1959 visit to Copenhagen went badly, and he left physics for defense operations research.
- **DeWitt** (*Physics Today*, 1970) popularized the view as **"many worlds"**. The image of the universe *splitting* is DeWitt's framing, not Everett's, who wrote of relative states and branches of the observer's memory.

**Standard criticisms:**
1. **The preferred-basis problem.** The relative-state expansion works in *any* basis of S₁. Everett picks the basis using the measurement interaction (the "good observation"). That begs the question of what makes a physical process a measurement. The standard modern answer is **decoherence** and **einselection** (Zeh 1970, Zurek), which we discussed under GRW.
2. **The probability problem.** If every branch occurs, it is unclear what "probability" means. Everett's measure argument shows that |a|² is the *natural* additive measure, but not why an observer should *expect* outcomes in proportion to it. Later attempts:
   - Deutsch (1999) and Wallace: a **decision-theoretic** derivation;
   - Zurek: **envariance**;
   - Sebens & Carroll: **self-locating uncertainty**;
   - Barrett: reading Everett's argument as a **typicality** claim, which is close to what Everett actually wrote.
3. **Personal identity.** It is unclear what it means for "me" to be in many branches.

## You

Is Everette's relative state related to the density matrix of a subsystem?

## Claude

# Relative states and the reduced density matrix

**Yes, closely.** Everett's relative states are the **conditional states** of a subsystem. The reduced density matrix is its **marginal (unconditional) state**. The reduced density matrix is the Born-weighted mixture of the relative states, for every choice of basis on the other subsystem.

The analogy is a joint probability distribution: relative states correspond to the conditional distributions p(y | x), and the density matrix to the marginal p(y) = Σₓ p(x) p(y | x).

---

## 1. The exact relation

Take a pure state ψ of S₁ + S₂ and any orthonormal basis {ξᵢ} of S₁.

**Unnormalized relative states.** Project out S₁ (a partial inner product):

  ψ̃ᵢ = (⟨ξᵢ| ⊗ 𝟙) ψ ∈ H₂,  with pᵢ = ‖ψ̃ᵢ‖² = |aᵢ|².

Normalizing gives Everett's relative state, ψ_rel[ξᵢ] = ψ̃ᵢ / √pᵢ, and

  ψ = Σᵢ ξᵢ ⊗ ψ̃ᵢ = Σᵢ aᵢ ξᵢ ⊗ ψ_rel[ξᵢ].

**The reduced density matrix.** Trace out S₁, using the same basis to compute the trace:

  ρ₂ = Tr₁ |ψ⟩⟨ψ| = Σᵢ (⟨ξᵢ| ⊗ 𝟙) |ψ⟩⟨ψ| (|ξᵢ⟩ ⊗ 𝟙) = **Σᵢ pᵢ |ψ_rel[ξᵢ]⟩⟨ψ_rel[ξᵢ]|**.

So ρ₂ is the mixture of relative states weighted by the Born probabilities of the corresponding S₁ outcomes. The partial trace does not depend on the basis, so **every basis of S₁ gives a decomposition of the same ρ₂**.

## 2. What each object keeps and loses

| | Relative states {ψ_rel[ξᵢ]} | Reduced density matrix ρ₂ |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of object | a vector, *conditional* on a state ξᵢ of S₁ | an operator, unconditional |
| Depends on a choice of S₁ basis? | **yes** | no |
| Encodes correlations with S₁? | yes, together with ψ you can recover the whole state | no: it is all of S₂'s local statistics and nothing more |
| Pure or mixed | pure (for a pure global state) | mixed whenever S₁ and S₂ are entangled |

**Different bases give different decompositions.**
- The relative states belonging to a general basis of S₁ are **not orthogonal** to each other.
- Changing the basis of S₁ changes them, but their mixture is always the same ρ₂.

**The Schmidt basis gives the eigen-decomposition.** Write ψ in Schmidt form, ψ = Σₖ √λₖ uₖ ⊗ vₖ. If ξᵢ = uₖ, the relative states are the vₖ, which are orthonormal. They are the **eigenvectors of ρ₂**, with weights λₖ equal to its **eigenvalues**. This is the special case where the "branches" of S₂ are perfectly distinguishable. It is unique when the eigenvalues are nondegenerate.

**Every decomposition arises this way.** Schrödinger (1935–36) and Hughston, Jozsa & Wootters (1993) proved that every decomposition of ρ₂ into an ensemble of pure states can be obtained as relative states, by conditioning on outcomes of some measurement on S₁.
- If the measurement is a PVM (an orthonormal basis), the ensembles have at most dim H₁ members.
- If POVMs are allowed, every ensemble occurs.

This is **steering**: whoever controls S₁ chooses *which* decomposition of ρ₂ becomes real, by choosing what to measure. ρ₂ itself cannot change, which is why steering can't be used to signal.

## 3. An operator version (map–state duality)

Read ψ as a linear map Ψ : H₁* → H₂, with Ψ(ξ̄) = (⟨ξ| ⊗ 𝟙)ψ. Then:
- the **relative-state map** is just ξ ↦ Ψ(ξ̄);
- **ρ₂ = ΨΨ†** and **ρ₁ = (Ψ†Ψ)ᵀ**;
- the **Schmidt decomposition** is the **singular value decomposition** of Ψ.

This is the Choi–Jamiołkowski duality from the formalization thread (A ⊸ B ≅ A\* ⊗ B). In that language, Everett's relative state is ψ viewed as a *process* from S₁ to S₂, and ρ₂ is that process composed with its adjoint, which forgets which input was used.

## 4. Generalization: quantum conditional states

Everett defined relative states only for a **pure** global state and a **vector** of S₁. Both restrictions can be dropped. For a mixed global state ρ₁₂ and any effect E (from a POVM) on S₁:

  ρ₂|E = Tr₁[(E ⊗ 𝟙) ρ₁₂] / Tr[(E ⊗ 𝟙) ρ₁₂].

Then:
- ρ₂ = Σᵢ p(Eᵢ) ρ₂|Eᵢ for any POVM {Eᵢ}, which is the law of total probability in quantum form;
- the conditional states are generally mixed.

Leifer & Spekkens ("Towards a formulation of quantum theory as a causally neutral theory of Bayesian inference", 2013) develop this into a quantum analogue of conditional probability. This connects Everett's construction to the QBist thread: as mathematics, a relative state is a **conditional state**. The interpretations differ about what it means:
- Everett: an objective branch;
- QBism: the agent's updated beliefs.

## 5. Why Everett needs more than ρ₂

The relationship also shows where the interpretive weight lies.

- **Improper mixtures.** For an entangled state, ρ₂ looks like a mixture of the vₖ, but nothing in the theory says that S₂ "really is" in one of them. D'Espagnat called this an **improper mixture**, as opposed to a **proper mixture**, which describes ignorance of an actual state.
  - Reading ρ₂ as ignorance of the outcome is exactly the step that collapse makes and that Everett rejects.
  - Everett keeps the *whole* ψ: every relative state is equally actual, each relative to its own memory state.
- **Basis-dependence is the preferred-basis problem.** ρ₂ has infinitely many decompositions. Calling some of them "branches" requires picking one. Everett picks the basis that the observer's **memory** becomes correlated with in a "good observation".
- **Decoherence makes this choice physical.**
  - When the environment records the apparatus pointer, the reduced density matrix of system + apparatus becomes **diagonal in the pointer basis**, and that basis stays stable over time.
  - The branches are then the relative states *with respect to environmental records*.
  - Equivalently, the Schmidt decomposition with respect to the environment stabilizes on the pointer basis. This is Zurek's einselection.

---

**Summary.** The reduced density matrix is the average of Everett's relative states, and it is the same whatever basis you condition on. Everett's distinctive move is to keep the conditional structure that ρ₂ throws away, and to treat every term in the decomposition as actual, relative to the observer state it is correlated with. In mathematical terms, a relative state is a **quantum conditional state** and ρ₂ is a **marginal**.

**Note on sources:** the derivation above is standard. I'm not certain how far Everett's own texts go here. I believe the 1955–56 long thesis uses subsystem density matrices and an information-theoretic measure of correlation, and the 1957 paper works only with relative state vectors, but I can't check either text from here.

## Claude

# How a unitary can record a measurement

**Short answers:**
1. **A unitary can record.** Recording is **copying into a blank register**, and that is reversible. What is irreversible is **erasing** or overwriting a record, not writing it.
2. **One premise needs correcting:** the system *does* change. Its reduced state loses its coherences.
3. **What stops recording:** in the textbook model, nothing internal. The coupling is switched off by hand. In a closed universe the "switch" has to be another physical degree of freedom, such as a particle flying out of range.
4. **How many records:** finitely many at any time. At most countably many perfectly distinguishable records can exist in any separable Hilbert space. **Uncountability** shows up in a different place: the *set of complete branch histories* over infinite time is uncountable, and that is why Everett needs a measure on branches.

---

## 1. Why the recording map is unitary

### The map only needs to be an isometry on the relevant inputs
Everett's "good observation" fixes the action only on states where the memory starts **blank**, in a state m₀:

  φᵢ ⊗ m₀ ↦ φᵢ ⊗ mᵢ.

**The inputs are orthonormal:** ⟨φᵢ⊗m₀, φⱼ⊗m₀⟩ = δᵢⱼ.

**So are the outputs:** ⟨φᵢ⊗mᵢ, φⱼ⊗mⱼ⟩ = δᵢⱼ⟨mᵢ, mⱼ⟩ = δᵢⱼ. The δᵢⱼ comes from the system factor, so this holds even if the records mᵢ are not orthogonal to each other.

The map is therefore an isometry from span{φᵢ ⊗ m₀} into H_S ⊗ H_M. Any isometry between subspaces extends to a unitary on the whole space, in infinite dimensions provided the orthogonal complements have the same dimension. The unitary may do anything at all to non-blank memory states, as long as it stays unitary.

### Concrete forms
- **Controlled unitary:** U = Σᵢ Pᵢ ⊗ Vᵢ, where the Vᵢ are unitaries on the memory with Vᵢ m₀ = mᵢ. Its inverse is U† = Σᵢ Pᵢ ⊗ Vᵢ†.
- **CNOT** (Controlled-NOT, for a qubit): |i⟩|m⟩ ↦ |i⟩|m ⊕ i⟩, where ⊕ is addition mod 2. If m = 0, this copies i into the memory. If the memory already holds something, it XORs (adds mod 2) rather than overwriting, which is what keeps it reversible.
- **Von Neumann pointer:** U = exp(−i g τ A ⊗ p̂/ħ), with p̂ the pointer's momentum. It shifts the pointer position by g τ aᵢ in the branch where A has value aᵢ.

### What the intuition gets wrong
- **"The system doesn't change."** This is true only for eigenstates. A superposition Σ aᵢ φᵢ becomes entangled with the memory, so the system's reduced density matrix loses its off-diagonal terms: Σ aᵢ āⱼ |φᵢ⟩⟨φⱼ| becomes Σ |aᵢ|² |φᵢ⟩⟨φᵢ|. That is a real change. It is the decoherence that measurement causes, and the reason the system looks collapsed (see the relative-state/density-matrix answer).
- **"Changing the observer depending on the system is irreversible."** Copying information into a known blank register loses no information, so it is logically reversible (Landauer 1961; Bennett 1973, 1982). U† *un-records*. What costs entropy is **erasure**: mapping an unknown record back to m₀.
- **The real constraint is no-cloning.** The copy works only for the **orthogonal** states φᵢ. Copying an arbitrary unknown state would be cloning, which is impossible. So a recording interaction can copy in one basis only, and it singles out that basis. This is the preferred-basis problem seen from the side of the apparatus.

---

## 2. Where the irreversibility actually comes from

The recording unitary can be reversed in principle. In practice it is effectively irreversible ("for all practical purposes", Bell's FAPP), for three reasons:

- **Redundancy.** A real record is not one register. Photons, air molecules and the apparatus's thermal degrees of freedom hold many copies of it. Zurek calls this **quantum Darwinism**. Undoing the measurement requires reversing *every* copy coherently.
- **Recurrence times.** For a large environment, the time for the dynamics to bring the system back near its starting state is astronomically long.
- **Thermodynamics.** Keeping a finite memory working means erasing old records. Erasure moves entropy to the environment (at least k_B ln 2 per bit, Landauer's bound), which spreads the information further.

Reversibility in principle is exactly what **Wigner's-friend no-go theorems** use. Frauchiger–Renner and Bong et al. (Local Friendliness) have Wigner *undo* the friend's measurement. Small-scale experiments, where the "friend" is a photon's path or polarization degree of freedom, do this (Proietti et al., *Science Advances*, 2019). These "friends" are tiny, unredundant records, the opposite of a brain.

---

## 3. What makes recording stop

**In the textbook model, nothing.** The coupling is time-dependent, H(t) = g(t) A ⊗ p̂, with g(t) ≠ 0 only during [0, τ]. The switch is controlled from outside the system, which a description of the whole universe cannot have.

**What happens if the coupling never switches off?** It depends on the memory:
- **Continuous pointer:** the shift keeps growing, x ↦ x + g t aᵢ. The record keeps being reinforced and stays consistent, because the eigenvalue doesn't change. The system is held in place (the quantum Zeno effect).
- **Finite memory:** the record oscillates. With H = g P₁ ⊗ σ_x, where σ_x is the Pauli-X operator, the memory is fully recorded at t = π/(2g) and back to *blank* at t = π/g, like Rabi oscillation. Applying CNOT twice erases the record in the same way. **So the duration matters**, and "stop at the right moment" is part of the measurement.

**In a closed system the stop has to be internal.** The total Hamiltonian is time-independent. Time dependence appears *relative to* another degree of freedom:
- **Scattering kinematics:** H = V(x_particle − x_detector) A ⊗ p̂. The interaction is on only while the particle's wave packet overlaps the detector, and it ends when the particle flies away. Most real measurements stop this way.
- **A clock subsystem:** in the Page–Wootters mechanism, time evolution is a correlation with a clock, and "the coupling is on" is a condition on the clock's state.
- **Dissipation:** the record is amplified into many degrees of freedom (as in a photomultiplier avalanche) and moves irreversibly to an environment. The dynamics cannot practically "un-record", so stopping no longer needs to be precisely timed.

**Everett's sequential memory needs one more mechanism.** For ψ^O[A, B, C, …], each observation must write into the *next blank slot*. That needs a **counter**, like a Turing-machine head: a physical degree of freedom that advances unitarily (a shift operator) after each recording. Everett's model assumes this structure exists; it doesn't derive it.

So the question of when recording stops does not disappear in Everett's account. It moves into the **dynamics of the interaction**, including its timing and spatial range. In von Neumann's account the same question was hidden in the choice of where to put the Heisenberg cut.

---

## 4. How many records?

### At a given time, in one branch: finitely many
- **Separability.** In a separable Hilbert space, any set of mutually orthogonal unit vectors is at most **countable**. So perfectly distinguishable memory configurations, and hence possible records, are at most countably many. An uncountable family of perfectly distinguishable records would need a non-separable Hilbert space.
- **Finite size and energy.** A memory of bounded size and energy effectively uses a finite-dimensional subspace. The **Bekenstein bound** S ≤ 2πk_B R E/(ħc) limits the information in a region of radius R containing energy E, so the number of records is **finite**.
- **Continuous pointers do not escape this.** The "records" |x⟩ of a position pointer are not normalizable vectors. As in the earlier answer on smeared position, real readouts are unsharp POVMs with finite resolution. Over a bounded range only finitely many readings can be told apart, about range/resolution, and fewer still at finite energy.

### Over a lifetime, in one branch: finite, and countable at most
- **Speed limit.** The Margolus–Levitin theorem says a system with mean energy E above its ground state needs at least time πħ/(2E) to reach an orthogonal state. So a recording rate is bounded, and a **finite lifetime gives finitely many records**.
- **Infinite lifetime with discrete events:** countably many.
- **Continuous monitoring** produces a record that is a function of time, such as a measurement current I(t). This looks like a continuum of records, but at finite bandwidth it carries finitely many independent values per unit time (Nyquist–Shannon). It is a countable sequence in disguise.
- **The memory forgets.** The memory is finite and the records keep coming, so the observer must overwrite. The *current* memory is a bounded window, and each erasure moves the information into the environment (Landauer). The **branch** then continues to be defined by environmental records even after the observer has forgotten.

### Across all branches, over all time: this is where uncountability appears
- Suppose there are infinitely many binary recordings. Each complete branch history is then an infinite sequence in {0,1}^ℕ, the **Cantor space**, which has **2^ℵ₀ elements, uncountably many** (ℵ₀ is the cardinality of the natural numbers).
- At every *finite* time there are only finitely many branches. The **set of complete histories** is uncountable all the same.
- Any single infinite history has Born measure **zero**. That is why Everett's probability statement has to be a **measure-theoretic** one:
  - put the product of the |aᵢ|² measures on the Cantor space of histories;
  - the strong law of large numbers then says that the set of histories whose frequencies match the Born rule has **measure 1**.
  
  This is the precise content of Everett's "for almost all branches".

### Counting branches is not well defined anyway
Branches are defined only after a **coarse-graining**: decoherence gives *approximately* orthogonal sectors, at a chosen resolution. With continuous variables, refining the resolution gives more branches, with no natural stopping point. Wallace argues that "the number of branches" has no meaning and that only the **measure** does. This is the branch-level version of the earlier point that records must be unsharp.

How can the arrow of time be explained in this picture?

## Claude

# The arrow of time in Everett's picture

**Short answer:** the Everett picture has no time asymmetry in its *laws*. Everett removed the one time-asymmetric element of quantum mechanics, collapse (Process 1). So any arrow has to come from the **state**, specifically from a **low-entanglement initial condition**. Given that condition, the thermodynamic arrow, the arrow of decoherence and branching, and the arrow of records and memory all point the same way, because they are the same process: **correlations building up from an uncorrelated start**.

Using the recording model from the last answer: **the future is the direction in which blank registers are used up.**

---

## 1. The puzzle
- **Unitary dynamics is time-symmetric.**
  - Every U(t) has an inverse U(−t).
  - The Schrödinger equation is invariant under the antiunitary time-reversal operator T (up to the tiny T-violation in weak interactions, seen in kaons and B mesons, which plays no role in the macroscopic arrow).
- **Collapse was the asymmetric ingredient.**
  - Process 1 maps a pre-measurement state to a post-measurement state, never the other way round.
  - Standard QM is asymmetric in the same way: it has *pre*-selection (state preparation) but no *post*-selection, and the Born rule predicts but does not retrodict in the same form.
  - The **two-state vector formalism** (Aharonov, Bergmann & Lebowitz, 1964) restores the symmetry by adding a final boundary state alongside the initial one. Its ABL rule (named after the three authors) gives probabilities for measurements between the two boundary states. This shows the asymmetry comes from the boundary conditions, not the laws.
- **Everett keeps only Process 2.** So the arrow must come from the initial state of the universal wavefunction.

## 2. Three arrows reduced to one

| Arrow | In Everett's picture | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Records / memory** (psychological) | memories hold the past, not the future | recording copies information into **blank** registers |
| **Decoherence / branching** | branches split toward the future and don't merge | entanglement with the environment grows from a low initial value |
| **Thermodynamic** | entropy increases | the entanglement (and coarse-grained) entropy of subsystems grows |

The three are one process seen in different ways:
- **The universe as a whole stays pure.** The von Neumann entropy of the universal state is constant (zero).
- **Subsystems become entangled.** The entanglement entropy of a subsystem, S(ρ_sub) = −Tr ρ_sub log ρ_sub, starts low and grows.
- **The effective number of branches** roughly tracks exp(entanglement entropy).

So branching *is* entropy growth, seen through relative states.

## 3. Applying this to the recording model

From the last answer: the record φᵢ ⊗ m₀ ↦ φᵢ ⊗ mᵢ is unitary only because the memory starts in a known **blank** state m₀.

- **Blank registers are a low-entropy resource.** A pure, known, uncorrelated state is exactly what thermodynamics calls low entropy. Bennett and Landauer showed that a blank tape is "fuel" for computation.
- **Recording consumes blanks.** Each recording makes a register correlated with the system, so it is no longer blank.
- **Forgetting dumps entropy.** A finite memory has to erase, and erasure sends at least k_B ln 2 per bit into the environment.
- **Why memory records the past.** The past is the direction in which registers were blank. A memory of the "future" would need registers that are *already correlated* with systems they haven't interacted with yet. That is the time-reverse of recording, a precisely tuned state.
- **What stops recording, again.** Scattering was the physical "switch" in the last answer. It is asymmetric in the same way: **incoming** states are uncorrelated and **outgoing** states are correlated. Boltzmann's assumption of molecular chaos ("Stosszahlansatz") has the same structure, and it too is an assumption about initial conditions.

## 4. The initial condition

**Past Hypothesis** (Albert, *Time and Chance*, 2000): the universe began in a state of very low entropy. In Everett's setting this means **low entanglement**: roughly a product state, or close to one, with nothing already recorded anywhere.

**Zeh's version** (*The Physical Basis of the Direction of Time*, several editions from 1989):
- The quantum arrow comes from an initial condition with **no correlations**.
- This works like the Sommerfeld radiation condition in electrodynamics, which allows outgoing waves but no incoming ones. In the quantum case, the condition excludes the precisely tuned correlations that would make branches *recombine*.

**Why branches don't merge:**
- Merging (recoherence) is the time-reverse of branching.
- It is allowed by the dynamics. Reversing Wigner's friend is the in-principle version.
- But it needs every redundant copy of the record to be correlated in exactly the right way. Given the Past Hypothesis, such states are **exponentially rare**, for the same reason that broken eggs don't reassemble.

**Quantum cosmology:**
- In the **Wheeler–DeWitt equation**, the "Schrödinger equation of the whole universe" in canonical quantum gravity, there is no external time at all.
- Time emerges semiclassically, for example through the scale factor of the universe, or relationally through a clock subsystem, as in the Page–Wootters mechanism from last time.
- **Kiefer & Zeh** (*Phys. Rev. D*, 1995) proposed that the low-entanglement condition holds at **small scale factor**. The arrow then points in the direction of expansion. Their model also has a recollapsing universe, where the arrow is tied to the scale factor rather than to an external time. I recall this only roughly, so check it.

## 5. How other approaches locate the arrow

| Approach | Source of the arrow |
|---|---|
| **Everett** | the initial condition only (low entanglement) |
| **GRW/CSL** | partly the law: collapse is time-asymmetric. Albert (2000) proposed that GRW collapses could even *supply* the probabilities of statistical mechanics, though a Past Hypothesis is still needed |
| **Copenhagen / von Neumann** | Process 1, plus preparation without post-selection |
| **Two-state vector formalism** | symmetric laws with two boundary conditions; the arrow is the asymmetry between the initial and final states |
| **QBism** | the agent's standpoint: one bets on the future and acts into it |
| **Maccone (*PRL*, 2009)** | entropy-*decreasing* processes in quantum mechanics would also erase the memories that record them, so they can't be observed. This is disputed: Jennings & Rudolph (2010) argued it doesn't explain the arrow |

## 6. Open problems specific to Everett

1. **Circularity in probability.** Everett's measure on branches presupposes well-defined branches. Branches require decoherence, and decoherence requires the low-entropy initial condition. So the **Born-rule probabilities in Everett depend on the arrow**, and the explanation of the arrow uses probabilities ("exponentially rare"). Wallace and others accept this mutual dependence. Critics regard it as circular.
2. **Why the Past Hypothesis holds.** Everett does not explain it, any more than classical physics does. Proposals include inflation, a "no-boundary" initial state (Hartle–Hawking), and Penrose's Weyl-curvature hypothesis. None is settled.

# "Deliberation crowds out prediction"

## 1. The thesis
> An agent who is deliberating about an action cannot coherently assign a probability to that action, while the deliberation is going on.

The slogan comes from **Isaac Levi** (*Hard Choices*, 1986; *The Covenant of Reason*, 1997). **Wolfgang Spohn** made the same point in decision-theoretic form ("Where Luce and Krantz do really generalize Savage's decision model", *Erkenntnis*, 1977), and it is sometimes called "Spohn's principle": a decision model should not assign probabilities to the decision-maker's own acts. Earlier versions:
- **Ramsey**, in his 1929 notes, said roughly that from the agent's own point of view, a present voluntary action is not something to be predicted. I'm paraphrasing from memory.
- **Ginet**, "Can the will be caused?" (1962): you cannot know in advance what you will decide, because knowing it would mean you had already decided.
- **Kant**: we must act "under the idea of freedom", whatever theoretical reason says.

The claim is about the agent's **standpoint**. Others may assign probabilities to my acts, and so may I to my past acts or to acts I have already decided on. The exclusion applies to the **first person, in the present, for the act under deliberation**.

---

## 2. Four arguments for it

### (a) Betting (Levi)
Credences are defined operationally by betting rates. Suppose I'm offered a bet on "I will do A".
- I can **make the bet win or lose by choosing**, so my betting rate tells you about my preferences over bets, not my uncertainty about A.
- So there is no stable betting quotient, and no credence that a coherence (Dutch-book) argument could pin down.
- In QBist terms, a Dutch-book coherence argument only works for events the agent **does not control**.

### (b) Decision theory: credences about acts would make deliberation pointless
- In **Savage's** theory, acts are *functions* from states to outcomes, and probabilities are defined over states only. Acts are not events in the probability space.
- Suppose I nevertheless had credences P(A) in my own acts. Then:
  - if I am rational and know my own utilities, P(best act) → 1, and deliberation is already **finished**;
  - if P(A) is not near 1, my credences disagree with my own expected-utility calculation.
  
  Either way, the act-credences play no role in the decision.
- **Jeffrey's** evidential decision theory *does* use P(state | act), so it needs P(act) > 0. This is part of what Spohn objected to: an act should be modelled as an **intervention**, not as information you condition on.

### (c) Causal modelling: an act is an intervention (Pearl)
- **Observing** X = x gives P(y | x): learning about X tells you about its causes.
- **Intervening** with do(X = x) gives P(y | do(x)): every arrow *into* X is cut, and X is set from outside.
- An action, seen from the agent's side, is a do(): its causes are **removed from the model**. Without its causes the act has no distribution, so there is nothing to predict.
- Meek & Glymour ("Conditioning and intervening", *BJPS*, 1994) read the dispute between evidential and causal decision theory as a dispute over exactly this point.
- **Newcomb's problem** is the test case. A reliable predictor makes my act *evidence* about the state. If I treat my act as a prediction target, I one-box (choose only the opaque box), as evidential decision theory recommends. If I treat it as an intervention, I two-box (take both boxes), as causal decision theory recommends.

### (d) Self-reference
- **MacKay**'s "logical indeterminacy": suppose there were a prediction of my choice that is correct whether or not I believe it. If I believe it, I can act against it. So no prediction is binding *for me*, even in a deterministic world.
- **Popper's "Oedipus effect"**: announcing a prediction can change the outcome it predicts.
- This is the **diagonal** structure from your IDEA 2 regress, and Breuer's impossibility of complete self-measurement. A complete self-prediction would have to include itself.

---

## 3. Objections and refinements

**Rabinowicz** ("Does practical deliberation crowd out self-prediction?", *Erkenntnis*, 2002) separates three claims:
1. The agent **cannot** have act-credences.
2. The agent **should not** have them.
3. Act-credences may exist, but they are **idle**, playing no role in the choice.

Most defenders end up with (3).

**Joyce** (*Philosophical Studies*, 2002): the agent may hold act-credences that **change during deliberation**, but must not use them as *evidence* for the decision. Skyrms (*The Dynamics of Rational Deliberation*, 1990) models deliberation as a process in which act-credences evolve toward an **equilibrium**, at which point the agent acts. On this view the act-credences are not crowded out; they are what deliberation produces.

**Hájek** ("Deliberation welcomes prediction", 2016) gives counterexamples:
- **weakness of will**: I may reasonably predict that I won't stick to my diet;
- **plans**: I can predict that I'll take the 8:15 train, having decided to;
- **group agents**: a committee can predict its own vote;
- **mixed strategies** in game theory: a randomized choice *is* a probability over my acts.

**A refined version that survives these:**
> For the act now being deliberated, from the deliberator's standpoint, credences about the act cannot serve as **inputs** to the deliberation.

The past, other agents, settled plans and the third-person view are not affected. The thesis is **perspectival and indexical**, not metaphysical. It is compatible with determinism and with other agents predicting me perfectly, which makes it a kind of **compatibilism about one's own standpoint**.

---

## 4. Connections to physics

### 4a. Operational QM already builds this in
In every operational formulation (GPTs, Ludwig, Davies–Lewis, the Chiribella–D'Ariano–Perinotti framework), the basic object is

  p(outcome | setting).

The **setting** (which measurement, which preparation) is an **input label**, not a random variable. The theory doesn't assign it a probability.
- **Kochen–Specker contextuality** treats the context in the same way: as a parameter.
- **Quantum combs** (Chiribella, D'Ariano & Perinotti 2009) and **process matrices** (Oreshkov, Costa & Brukner 2012) make "an agent's intervention is an input" the basic structure.

So "deliberation crowds out prediction" is not a philosophical extra. It is how the **input/output form** of the operational formalism is set up.

### 4b. Bell's measurement independence
- Bell assumes p(settings | hidden variable λ) = p(settings): the settings are independent of λ.
- **Superdeterminism** denies this. It treats the experimenters' choices as *predictable from λ*.
- The thesis justifies measurement independence **from the experimenter's standpoint**: their own settings are interventions, not things they predict. It does not show that independence holds *objectively*. That is why superdeterminism cannot be refuted outright, but cannot be *used* by the agents inside it either.

### 4c. The cut and Wigner's friend
- This is the decision-theoretic form of your **role argument** (IDEA 2): the same thing cannot be, at the same moment, *the agent choosing* and *a system being predicted*.
- **Wigner** may assign probabilities to the friend's actions, because for Wigner the friend is a system. The **friend** cannot, for actions they are deliberating on.
- **Frauchiger–Renner** needs agents to reason about other agents' reasoning *as predictions*, including predictions that feed back to themselves. QBism's reply was to block the transfer of certainty between agents. This principle is a related constraint: it restricts what can be fed back into an agent's own deliberation.
- **Everett's** servomechanism observer has no deliberation. Its "choices" are fixed by the Hamiltonian, and only the third-person view exists. The thesis says the first-person view is not *derivable* from that third-person description, only consistent with it.

### 4d. The arrow of time (from the last answer)
- **Price's** causal perspectivalism: the asymmetry "we can affect the future, not the past" comes from the deliberator's standpoint. You may retrodict your past acts but not predict your current one.
- In terms of the recording model: a past act is **a record** (a filled register), and records can be conditioned on. The act under deliberation **has no record yet**, and predicting it would need a record of the future.

## 5. A formalization in the style of your intro/elim project

The thesis can be read as a **typing discipline**:

| Standpoint | Type of the epistemic state | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Agent, while deliberating | **A → Δ(O)**, a Markov kernel or channel | "if I do a, I expect outcomes distributed like this" |
| Observer of the agent | **Δ(A × O)**, a joint distribution | "the agent will do a with probability p, and then …" |
| Agent, after acting | **!A ⊗ Δ(O)** | the act is now *classical, copyable data*: a record |

Notation: Δ(X) is the set of probability distributions on X, and A → Δ(O) is a function from acts to distributions over outcomes. ⊗ and ! are as in the glossary.

- **Moving from kernel to joint requires a prior on A.** That prior is exactly what deliberation rules out from the inside, while an outside observer can supply it.
- **Intro/elim:**
  - *Introduction:* supposing "if I do a" introduces the act as a λ-bound **variable**, an input with no distribution.
  - *Elimination:* deciding applies the kernel to the chosen a.
  - *Promotion:* after acting, the act is promoted to **!A**, a record that can be copied and conditioned on.
- **Categorical setting:** in **Markov categories** (Fritz, 2020; Cho & Jacobs, 2019), the difference is between a morphism A → O (a kernel) and a state I → A ⊗ O (a joint distribution), where I is the monoidal unit. The thesis says the deliberating agent's credences are morphisms, not states.
- **Quantum version:** the agent's state is a **quantum comb** or **instrument** indexed by settings. The settings are classical labels, outside the domain of the Born rule.

This gives a precise version of IDEA 1's "freedom" rule:
> **An agent's own act enters its theory only as an abstraction (an input variable), never as a term with a distribution. It becomes a term only once it is recorded.**

Note what changes and what doesn't. Before the act, the type is A → Δ(O): no distribution on A. After it, the act is recorded data (!A) and the expectations are an ordinary distribution Δ(O). Nothing about my past acts is crowded out.

## 6. Assessment

**Strengths**
- It is the most formal handle on "freedom" in this whole thread. It needs no metaphysics, it is compatible with determinism, and it is already built into operational QM's input/output structure.
- It unifies several ideas: the role argument (IDEA 2), the arrow of agency, measurement independence, and the treatment of acts as interventions.
- It formalizes cleanly as a **typing rule** (kernel vs joint distribution, input vs record).

**Weaknesses and open questions**
- **Precise scope.** Hájek's counterexamples (weakness of will, plans) show that the exclusion must be limited to the act *currently under deliberation* and to its use *as evidence*. Is "deliberation" then just defined as whatever excludes act-credences? There is a risk of circularity.
- **Deliberation dynamics.** If Skyrms and Joyce are right, act-credences exist and *evolve* toward a choice. The kernel/joint split would then be the end of a process rather than a fixed type distinction.
- **Partial agents.** Groups, subagents and divided minds may predict "their" acts in part. This connects to your small-versus-large agency question (IDEA 3): how much of a system has to be engaged in deliberation for the exclusion to apply?
- **Ontic or merely perspectival?** The principle makes measurement independence *rational to assume* from inside an agent. It does not make it *true*, so it does not refute superdeterminism.
