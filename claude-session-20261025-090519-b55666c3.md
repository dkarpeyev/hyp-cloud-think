# How spectral theory comes out of QM

**Short answer:** Spectral theory comes from measurement, but not from the measurement *problem*. It is the dictionary that translates "a measurement with real-valued outcomes" into "an operator you can do algebra with". Non-commutativity is what makes that dictionary non-trivial. Each observable on its own is a classical random variable, but no single sample space holds all of them at once.

---

## 1. Three places it comes from

### (a) Historically, the spectrum was literal
In 1908 the Ritz combination principle observed that atomic spectral lines satisfy ν_{ij} = T_i − T_j. Lines are *differences* of terms, so they are indexed by **pairs** (i, j), not by single numbers. Heisenberg (1925) took such two-index arrays as the observables and found that they multiply like matrices, which do not commute. The terms T_i turned out to be the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian.

Hilbert had already named the eigenvalue set the "Spektrum" (around 1904–1912) for purely mathematical reasons. QM turned the metaphor into physics: **the observed light spectrum is built from the operator spectrum of H.** Von Neumann (1927–32) then extended the spectral theorem to unbounded operators because QM needed it: position, momentum and energy are all unbounded.

### (b) Conceptually, from measurement statistics
Start operationally. An observable A is a procedure that gives each state ρ a **probability measure on the outcome set ℝ**, and it is consistent with relabelling outcomes, so f(A) is measured by measuring A and then applying f. In Hilbert space, the natural object that does this is a **projection-valued measure (PVM)**: Borel sets Δ ↦ projections E(Δ), with
  p(A ∈ Δ | ρ) = Tr(ρ E(Δ)).

The **spectral theorem** says that PVMs on ℝ correspond one-to-one to self-adjoint operators:
  A = ∫ λ dE(λ),  E = E_A.

So the theorem is not an extra assumption. It says the *statistical* description (a PVM) and the *algebraic* description (an operator) carry the same information. You need the operator side to add observables, take commutators and generate dynamics. You need the PVM side to get probabilities.

- **σ(A)** is the set of possible outcomes, i.e. the support of E_A.
- Point spectrum gives discrete outcomes, and continuous spectrum gives outcomes like position.
- f(A) = ∫ f(λ) dE(λ) is the functional calculus. It is classical post-processing of the outcome.

### (c) Dynamically, from symmetry
**Stone's theorem:** strongly continuous one-parameter unitary groups U(t) correspond one-to-one to self-adjoint generators, U(t) = e^{−itH}. Here the spectral theorem acts as a Fourier analysis of the time evolution:
  ⟨ψ, U(t)ψ⟩ = ∫ e^{−itλ} dμ_ψ(λ).

- **Pure point spectrum:** the motion is quasi-periodic, so the state stays localized (bound states).
- **Absolutely continuous spectrum:** correlations decay (Riemann–Lebesgue), so the state spreads out (scattering).
- The **RAGE theorem** makes this split exact.

The same self-adjoint operator is both a **quantity you measure** and the **generator of a symmetry**, e.g. momentum and translations, or energy and time evolution. Spectral theory is the one theory that serves both roles.

## 2. Is it the measurement problem?

**No.** Spectral theory belongs to the measurement *postulate*, not the measurement *problem*.

| | What it says | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Spectral theorem + Born rule** | the possible outcomes are σ(A), with probabilities Tr(ρE(Δ)) | a theorem plus a postulate; every interpretation accepts it (Copenhagen, Bohm, Everett, QBism) |
| **Measurement problem** | why does a *single* outcome occur, and when do we switch from unitary evolution to E(Δ)-update? | open; it is where the interpretations disagree |

Put simply, the spectral decomposition lists the possible outcomes and their weights. The measurement problem asks why one of them actually happens.

There are three places where the two do meet:
1. **Eigenvalue–eigenstate link.** "A has value a exactly when ψ is an eigenvector." This reading of the spectral theorem is what creates the problem. Superpositions of pointer eigenstates have no value.
2. **Preferred basis.** A spectral decomposition gives you a basis for *each* observable. Nothing in the formalism says *which* observable the apparatus measures. Decoherence/einselection supplies that choice. Spectral theory doesn't.
3. **Everett (your MEAS thread).** The Schmidt decomposition of a system–apparatus state is the **spectral decomposition of the reduced density matrix**. The relative states are, in effect, its eigenvectors. If the spectrum is degenerate, the decomposition isn't unique. That is one technical form of the preferred-basis problem.

## 3. Non-commutativity: where the real content is

### (a) A single observable is classical
The C*-algebra generated by one self-adjoint A (and 1) is **commutative**. By **Gelfand duality**, every commutative C*-algebra is C(X) for a compact space X, its "spectrum". For C*(A), X = σ(A). So:

> **The spectral theorem is Gelfand duality applied to the commutative algebra generated by one observable.** Each observable on its own is a random variable on the sample space σ(A).

This also holds for any **commuting family**: they share a joint PVM on a joint spectrum, which acts as a classical sample space.

### (b) Classical mechanics is the case where everything commutes
Classical observables are functions on phase space Γ. All of them commute, so there is **one** spectrum, Γ itself, and one sample space for everything. States are probability measures on Γ.

### (c) Quantum mechanics: no global spectrum
If A and B don't commute, they have **no joint PVM**. There is no sample space on which both are random variables. Spectral theory still applies inside each commutative subalgebra (each **context**), but these local sample spaces can't be glued into one. That is:
- **Kochen–Specker:** there is no assignment of values to all observables that respects every context.
- **Isham–Butterfield / Döring–Isham:** the "spectral presheaf" over commutative subalgebras has **no global section**. This links to your TT thread and to IDEA 4's "contextuality as curvature".

So non-commutativity doesn't break spectral theory. It **breaks it into local pieces**: one classical probability space per context, with no global one.

### (d) Non-commutativity changes the values
In a commutative algebra, σ(A + B) ⊆ σ(A) + σ(B). In general this fails. For example:
- σ(σ_x) = σ(σ_z) = {±1}, but σ(σ_x + σ_z) = {±√2}, which is not a sum of possible values of each.

This is why von Neumann's 1932 no-hidden-variables assumption (that values add) was too strong (Bell, 1966). It also shows directly that **outcomes are not pre-existing values that add**.

### (e) Non-commutativity determines the spectra (quantization)
The commutation relations alone fix much of the spectrum:
- **[x, p] = iħ:** neither operator can be bounded (Wintner–Wielandt), so the spectral theorem for *unbounded* operators is required. Stone–von Neumann says the representation is unique, and that makes p's spectral measure the Fourier transform of x's.
- **Harmonic oscillator:** [a, a†] = 1 alone forces σ(N) = {0, 1, 2, …}, so the energies are ħω(n + ½).
- **Angular momentum:** [J_i, J_j] = iε_{ijk}J_k forces σ(J_z) ⊆ ½ℤ.
- **Uncertainty (Robertson):** Δ_ψA · Δ_ψB ≥ ½|⟨[A, B]⟩_ψ|. The spectral measures of non-commuting operators can't both be sharp in the same state.

So "quantum" in the sense of discrete is a **spectral consequence of non-commutativity**. Discreteness doesn't need to be postulated.

---

## 4. Summary

| Ingredient | What spectral theory does with it |
|---|---|
| Measurement with real outcomes | PVM ↔ self-adjoint operator (spectral theorem) |
| Symmetry and dynamics | unitary group ↔ generator (Stone); spectral type ↔ bound vs. scattering |
| A single observable or commuting family | a classical sample space σ(A) (Gelfand) |
| Non-commuting observables | no joint spectrum; only local, context-wise sample spaces (KS, spectral presheaf) |
| Commutation relations | determine the spectra themselves (quantization, uncertainty) |
| Measurement problem | *not* addressed; spectral theory gives the possible outcomes, not the selection of one |

**In one sentence:** QM is a non-commutative probability theory, and spectral theory is how each commutative part of it becomes ordinary probability.
