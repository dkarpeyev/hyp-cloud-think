## Setup

- **Turn tree T.** Nodes are turns. A turn's parent is the turn before it. Reopening a saved session and continuing it, or rewinding and re-asking, creates a **fork**. The distance between two turns, d_T, is the number of steps along the tree.
- **Semantic metric S.** d_S is the distance between text embeddings (for example, the arc-cosine of their similarity).
- **Goal.** Place every turn at a point x in the Poincaré disk 𝔻 so that the disk distance d_𝔻 tracks semantics, while keeping the **distortion of T** low.

Trees are where hyperbolic space is at its best. Any finite tree fits into ℍ² with distortion as close to 1 as you like (Sarkar 2011). Semantic metrics are usually not tree-like: their Gromov δ is large. **The two goals therefore compete, and the real design choice is how to trade one against the other.**

A useful fact: a linear conversation is a **path**, and a path fits exactly onto a geodesic. All of the tree distortion comes from **forks**, which is where history and semantics pull apart.

## Three designs, from strict to loose

### 1. Tree as a rigid skeleton, semantics only in the free choices (recommended)
Sarkar's construction leaves several things open, and semantics can decide all of them without hurting the tree:

| Free choice | What semantics decides |
|---|---|
| Rotation around each node (the order of its children) | Put semantically close branches next to each other on the circle. This is a circular ordering problem; a greedy TSP pass is enough. |
| Width of each child's angular cone | Make it proportional to the size or semantic spread of that child's subtree. |
| Edge length (scale τ) | Shorter edges inside a run of turns on the same topic, longer ones at a topic jump. Semantic distance then shows up as radial spacing. |
| Root and recentering | A Möbius transform that recenters the disk on the turn being viewed. This is an isometry, so it adds no distortion. |

The tree's worst-case distortion stays at 1+ε. Two turns that are close in meaning but far apart in history can't be close on the disk. Draw a **link between them** instead of moving the points.

### 2. Soft joint objective
Minimize a combined loss over x ∈ 𝔻ⁿ with Riemannian SGD, or work in the Lorentz model for numerical stability:

$$
\mathcal L = \lambda \sum_{(i,j)} \Big(\tfrac{d_{\mathbb D}(x_i,x_j)}{d_T(i,j)} - 1\Big)^2 \;+\; (1-\lambda)\, \mathcal L_{\text{sem}}(x)
$$

Here 𝓛_sem is either a stress term on d_S or a contrastive or t-SNE-style neighbor loss (CO-SNE, hyperbolic UMAP). Initialize from the design-1 layout. With λ ≈ 0.7–0.9 the tree mostly holds, and semantic clusters can bend branches toward each other.

### 3. Blended metric + h-MDS
Set d = λ·d_T + (1−λ)·d_S and run hyperbolic MDS (Sala et al. 2018). It's simple, but you lose any guarantee on tree distortion.

## Measuring "historical distortion"
Report these with λ as a slider:
- **Worst-case distortion:** maximum expansion × maximum contraction over pairs of turns.
- **Average distortion:** the mean of |d_𝔻/d_T − 1|.
- **MAP of tree neighbors:** for each turn, do its parent and children rank among its nearest points on the disk? (Nickel & Kiela 2017.)
- **Semantic side:** trustworthiness, or k-NN recall against d_S.

## Practical pitfalls
- **Precision.** Low-distortion tree embeddings need about depth × log(degree) bits. Long sessions push points toward the boundary, and float64 stops resolving beyond hyperbolic radius ≈ 38. Fixes: collapse each linear run of turns into one node at the coarse zoom level, use Lorentz coordinates, and recenter as the user navigates.
- **Stability.** When a turn is added, only its own subtree's cone should change. Design 1 has this locality built in. Designs 2 and 3 can move the whole layout, so warm-start them and add an anchoring penalty.
- **Two levels.** hyp currently embeds whole documents. The natural layering is to embed sessions semantically at the coarse level, then lay out each session's turn tree inside its own cone with design 1.

**My recommendation:** use design 1 as the default, because history is then preserved exactly. Use design 2 with a λ slider as an optional "semantic pull" view, and show the distortion figures alongside it so you can see the trade-off.

## Is history data or inductive bias? Both, at different levels

In the last answer, the **design 1** layout treated the turn tree as **inductive bias**: the geometry was hard-coded so that history *is* the structure. You can also treat the tree as **data**, meaning labeled examples of which thoughts relate to which. Valiant's framework shows where each belongs. (I found no Valiant notes in your THINK documents, so this draws on his published work.)

- **Inductive bias = the hypothesis class H.** Choosing hyperbolic geometry, low curvature, a hierarchical structure and a simple family of maps is the bias. It's fixed before any history is seen. In PAC terms, it's what makes learning possible from few examples.
- **Data = the historical edges.** Each parent→child turn, fork, reopen-and-continue, or document consulted in a session is an example of "related".

## Teacher vs. data
PAC learning assumes passive examples drawn i.i.d. from a distribution D. Your history isn't like that. **You chose** which session to reopen, where to fork and what to ask next. That's closer to Valiant's **learning from a teacher**. In *The Importance of Being Educable* (2024), he separates learning from experience from absorbing what one is told, and treats the ability to take in instruction as the distinctive human strength.

What this means here:

| | Passive data | Teacher signal |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Adjacency in time, co-occurrence in a session | Deliberate acts: reopening session X to continue it, forking, asking about a specific document |
| Sample efficiency | Needs many examples | Few: chosen examples carry more information (cf. *teaching dimension*, Goldman & Kearns) |
| Risk | Noise. Turns that are close in time aren't always close in meaning | You inherit the teacher's idiosyncrasies. Past paths may reflect accident or habit, not structure |

So give the deliberate acts more weight than incidental adjacency. Keep the semantic embedding as an independent check on the teacher.

## Turning this into a learner
Don't place points directly. Learn a map **f_θ : ℝᵈ (text embedding) → 𝔻** from the historical relationships:

1. **Training pairs:**
   - Positives: parent/child turns, sibling branches at a fork, a session and the documents it read. Your `.jsonl` transcripts record every `Read` and `Grep`, so document↔turn edges are already there.
   - Negatives: random pairs.
   - Weight each pair by teacher strength (deliberate > incidental).
2. **Loss:** a Poincaré contrastive or ranking loss (Nickel & Kiela style) on d_𝔻(f(a), f(b)), plus a semantic regularizer that keeps f close to plain semantic structure where history says nothing.
3. **Keep H small:** a linear map followed by the exponential map, or a tiny MLP. In the PAC view, few parameters means few edges are needed, and the map **generalizes**.
4. **Payoff:** f then places documents **with no history**, such as new PDFs and notes, according to *how you have related thoughts in the past*, not just by generic similarity. That is "learning from historical relationships" proper: history acts as supervision, not just as layout.
5. **Evaluation:** hold out some edges and check link prediction (MAP), tree distortion on the training edges, and semantic k-NN recall. Generalization to held-out edges is what separates learning from memorizing.

## Short answer
The **geometry is the bias**, the **history is the data**, and because you produced the data deliberately, it's **teaching rather than sampling**. That makes it sample-efficient but also partial, so it needs semantics as a regularizer.

One open question: do you want f to model only *your* way of relating thoughts (a personal teacher), or to stay close to general semantics? That choice sets the regularizer weight.
