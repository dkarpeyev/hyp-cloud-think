QBism fits in two places, and there is one place where your idea goes beyond it.

## 1. The update rule: QBism already says this

In QBism, collapse is an agent updating its beliefs after an experience. Your rule "collapse to whatever minimizes the update" matches a standard principle of Bayesian reasoning: **change your beliefs as little as the new data allows.** There are theorems for this in the quantum case:

- **[Hellmann, Kamiński & Kostecki (NJP 2016)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.7766)** prove that the von Neumann–Lüders collapse rule gives the unique post-measurement state that maximises relative entropy with respect to the prior state, among states consistent with the outcome. In plain terms, it is the smallest belief change that fits the outcome.
- **[Kostecki (arXiv:1408.3502)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.3502)** extends this to von Neumann algebras and to a quantum version of Jeffrey's rule (updating on uncertain evidence).

So once an outcome has happened, the "minimal update" half of your idea is ordinary QBism and has a theorem behind it.

## 2. Limited memory: QBism's one objective property

QBism says a quantum state is not a property of the system. It belongs to the agent. Fuchs makes one exception. He treats the **Hilbert-space dimension d** as something a system holds "all by itself", the way mass is in Newtonian mechanics. He calls d the system's capacity to respond when touched ([Fuchs & Stacey, as quoted in arXiv:1708.02148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02148)). I only found this in secondary sources, so check his wording before quoting it.

That is a close match for your memory capacity, with C = log₂ d. On this reading, the one thing QBism lets a quantum own is the size of its memory.

## 3. Where you go beyond QBism: choosing the outcome

QBism says nothing about **which** outcome happens. To a QBist, an outcome is something new created in the encounter. Probabilities are the agent's beliefs, not tendencies of the system. Your claim is different: the quantum **prefers** outcomes that are cheap to record. That claim is about dynamics, not belief, and there are two ways to read it.

- **Cost = surprisal.** If the update cost is −log p, then "preferring cheap updates" just restates the Born rule. Seen from inside the agent, it is close to empty: an agent expects most strongly the outcomes it already expects. This version adds nothing new and is fully QBist.
- **Any other cost.** Then your model has real tendencies in it, closer to Smolin's precedence principle than to QBism. QBism would reject it, and it makes predictions you could test.

## 4. Whose memory is it?

There is a real conflict here. QBism puts the state in the agent, while you put the memory in the quantum. One way to reconcile them is to give each quantum two different things:

| | Belongs to | QBism's view |
|---|---|---|
| ψ assigned **to** quantum q by an agent A | A | A's beliefs about q |
| memory M **of** quantum q | q | q's own "beliefs" about whatever it has interacted with |

Your model then becomes **QBism with every quantum as an agent**, where the excitation is the agent's record of its past. Fuchs keeps "agent" mostly undefined and mostly human. Making every quantum an agent is the step he avoids.

People have already taken that step:

- **Chris Fields**, ["Autonomy all the way down"](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1108.2024) (a critique of QBism), argues that QBism's formalism quietly assumes systems with objective dynamics.
- **[Fields, Friston, Glazebrook & Levin (2022)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.15242)**, "A free energy principle for generic quantum systems", treats **every** quantum system as an observer with finite memory that minimises variational free energy. That quantity is an upper bound on surprisal, or prediction error. This is very close to your proposal: a generic quantum, finite memory, and behaviour that minimises updates. I haven't read the full paper, so I can't say how much of the outcome choice it actually derives.

## Summary

- **The update rule** is QBism, backed by the max-relative-entropy theorems.
- **The memory capacity** is QBism's dimension d.
- **Giving each quantum its own memory** is "QBism all the way down", the line Fields takes.
- **The outcome preference** is outside QBism. It is either the Born rule in other words or a testable departure from it.
