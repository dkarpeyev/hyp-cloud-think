## 1. The one identity

Model: $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$. We want $\max_\theta \log p_\theta(x)$, but $\log p_\theta(x) = \log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz$ is intractable.

For **any** distribution $q(z)$:

$$
\log p_\theta(x) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z)}\right]}_{\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)\ =\ \text{ELBO}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)}_{\ge 0,\ \text{the gap}}
$$

To check it, substitute $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(z\mid x)\,p_\theta(x)$ inside the log. The $\log p_\theta(x)$ term comes out, and what's left is $-\mathrm{KL}$. You don't need Jensen's inequality. It's an exact decomposition, and the bound follows because KL ≥ 0.

The ELBO can be written two useful ways:

$$
\mathcal{L}(q,\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)]}_{\text{EM's }Q\text{-function}} + \underbrace{H(q)}_{\text{entropy}}
\;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z))}_{\text{regularizer}}
$$

The first form is the EM view. The second is the VAE view.

## 2. EM is coordinate ascent on $\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)$

This is the Neal & Hinton (1998) view:

- **E-step:** maximize $\mathcal{L}$ over $q$ with $\theta$ fixed. Since $\log p_\theta(x)$ doesn't depend on $q$, this is the same as minimizing the KL gap. The answer is $q = p_\theta(z\mid x)$, the gap is 0, and the bound touches $\log p_\theta(x)$.
- **M-step:** maximize $\mathcal{L}$ over $\theta$ with $q$ fixed. $H(q)$ is now a constant, so you maximize $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)]$, which is exactly the classical $Q(\theta\mid\theta_{\text{old}})$.

Monotone improvement comes out immediately:

$$
\log p_{\theta_{new}}(x) \ge \mathcal{L}(q,\theta_{new}) \ge \mathcal{L}(q,\theta_{old}) = \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x).
$$

Picture it as a family of lower bounds on the log-likelihood. The E-step lifts the bound until it touches the curve at $\theta_{old}$. The M-step climbs the bound.

**Sanity check with a Gaussian mixture.** $z$ is the component label. The exact posterior is the "responsibilities", and the M-step is weighted MLE. Everything is in closed form, so the gap is exactly 0 after every E-step.

## 3. From EM to VAE: three relaxations

| | Classical EM | Variational EM | VAE |
|---|---|---|---|
| $q$ | exact posterior $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ | best $q$ in a restricted family $\mathcal{Q}$ (e.g. mean-field) | $q_\phi(z\mid x)$, a neural net: **amortized** |
| E-step | closed form, gap = 0 | inner optimization, gap > 0 | one forward pass of the encoder |
| M-step | closed-form argmax | closed-form or gradient | a gradient step on θ |
| Schedule | alternate | alternate | **joint** SGD on $(\theta,\phi)$ |

1. **Intractable posterior → restrict q.** With a nonlinear decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ (a neural net), $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ has no closed form. So you optimize $q$ over a family $\mathcal{Q}$, typically diagonal Gaussians. The E-step becomes a KL projection onto $\mathcal{Q}$, and the gap no longer closes. You are now maximizing a bound, not the likelihood.
2. **Per-datapoint q → amortized q.** Classical variational EM keeps separate parameters $(\mu_i,\sigma_i)$ for every data point $x_i$ and optimizes each one. The VAE replaces all of these with one function $q_\phi(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2_\phi(x))$, the **encoder**. This adds a second gap, the **amortization gap**: the encoder may not output the best $q\in\mathcal{Q}$ for a given $x$.
3. **Alternation → joint stochastic gradients.** Rather than running each step to convergence, take one gradient step on $\phi$ (a partial E-step) and one on $\theta$ (a partial M-step, as in generalized EM). Do this simultaneously, on minibatches.

So the **decoder** $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ plays the role of the model being fit in the M-step. The **encoder** $q_\phi$ is a learned, approximate E-step.

## 4. Why the reparameterization trick is needed

The θ-gradient is easy, because $q$ doesn't depend on θ:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x,z)].$$

You estimate it with Monte Carlo samples, just like a stochastic M-step.

The φ-gradient is the hard part, because φ sets the distribution you're averaging over. Write $z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x)\odot\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. Then the expectation is over a fixed distribution, so the gradient can go inside it and through the sample, giving a low-variance estimator. The alternative is the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator, which works but has much higher variance. Classical EM never faces this problem, because its E-step is solved analytically rather than by gradient.

## 5. The global picture: alternating KL projections

Average over the data distribution and define two joint distributions on $(x,z)$:

- the **data side** $q(x,z) = p_{\text{data}}(x)\,q_\phi(z\mid x)$
- the **model side** $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)$

Then

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\mathcal{L}] = -\,\mathrm{KL}\big(q(x,z)\,\|\,p_\theta(x,z)\big) - H(p_{\text{data}}).
$$

So maximizing the ELBO means minimizing one KL divergence between these two joints, alternately over each side. That is Csiszár–Tusnády alternating minimization, which Amari calls the "em algorithm" in information geometry. The E-step projects onto the data side and the M-step projects onto the model side. EM is the case where the data-side family is rich enough to contain the true posterior. The VAE is the case where both families are parameterized by neural nets.

## 6. Consequences of a gap that doesn't close

- **The model gets biased toward easy posteriors.** θ is chosen to maximize $\log p_\theta(x) - \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x))$. So training prefers decoders whose true posterior is close to Gaussian. Approximate inference regularizes the model, sometimes helpfully and sometimes not. Classical EM has no such effect.
- **The KL direction makes q mode-seeking.** The gap is $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$, which penalizes $q$ for putting mass where $p$ has little. So a single-Gaussian $q$ settles on one mode of a multimodal posterior and underestimates variance.
- **Posterior collapse.** In the VAE form, a decoder strong enough to model $x$ without using $z$ can set $q_\phi(z\mid x)=p(z)$. That makes the KL term 0 and the latent unused. The bound can then be fine while the representation is useless. Common fixes are KL annealing, free bits, and β-VAE (which deliberately reweights the KL term).
- **Tightening the bound.** IWAE uses $\mathbb{E}\big[\log \tfrac1K\sum_k p(x,z_k)/q(z_k)\big]$. As $K\to\infty$ this approaches $\log p(x)$, effectively a better E-step done by importance sampling. Richer families for q, such as normalizing flows, shrink the approximation gap instead.

## Summary

In both methods the training objective is $\log p_\theta(x) - \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,\text{posterior})$.

- **EM** makes the KL term exactly zero with an analytic E-step, then maximizes over θ.
- **A VAE** can't make it zero. It learns a network that keeps the KL term small for every $x$, and it trains that network jointly with θ by stochastic gradients.

If it would help, I can work through a case where both are computable side by side, such as factor analysis or probabilistic PCA. There the exact E-step is Gaussian, so you can see EM and a linear VAE reach the same solution.

## Claude

Most of this is right: the channel framing, (*), the θ-claim, and the algebra in (**). Two things are off, and fixing them answers your question about bits-back.

## Small typos

- The corrections cost $-\ln p_\theta(x \mid z)$, not $-p_\theta(x \mid z)$.
- The total should be $-\ln p_\theta(x \mid \mathrm{enc}(x)) - \ln p(\mathrm{enc}(x))$, with enc(x) in both places.

## Correction 1: the MDL formula

$$
-\ln p_\theta(x) \;\neq\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}\big[-\ln p_\theta(x \mid z) - \ln p(z)\big]
$$

The log of an average is not the average of the logs:

$$
-\ln p_\theta(x) = -\ln \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p(z)}\, p_\theta(x \mid z)
$$

Your right-hand side is larger. Jensen's inequality gives the first part, and the prior entropy $H(p(z))$ is added on top. The exact statement uses the posterior, and it holds for every single z, not just on average:

$$
-\ln p_\theta(x) = -\ln p_\theta(x,z) + \ln p_\theta(z \mid x) \quad \text{for every } z
$$

## Correction 2: VE is not the negative ELBO

Your VE leaves out the entropy term:

$$
\text{VE} = \mathbb{E}_q[-\ln p_\theta(x,z)]
$$

$$
-\text{ELBO} = F = \mathbb{E}_q[-\ln p_\theta(x,z)] - H(q) = \text{VE} - H(q)
$$

Your own decomposition (**) shows what goes wrong:

$$
\text{VE} = -\ln p_\theta(x) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big) + H(q)
$$

$$
F = -\ln p_\theta(x) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)
$$

When you minimize over φ:

- **VE** is linear in q: it's the expectation of a fixed function, $-\ln p_\theta(z \mid x)$. A linear function on the simplex is minimized at a vertex, so the minimizer is a point mass on the posterior mode:

$$
\hat z(x) = \arg\max_z p_\theta(z \mid x)
$$

  So VE favors **deterministic** encoders. A stochastic encoder never beats the best deterministic one on VE. That contradicts your guess that "on average I can send fewer bits with a stochastic enc".
- **F** is minimized at $q = p_\theta(z \mid x)$, where it equals the MDL $-\ln p_\theta(x)$ exactly.

**The subtracted $H(q)$ is the bits-back term.** F is what you pay with bits-back. VE is what you pay without it.

This also fits the EM picture:

- Minimizing VE over θ is the M-step. You're right about that part: VE and F differ by $H(q)$, which doesn't depend on θ.
- Minimizing VE over q gives **hard EM**, also called Viterbi EM. For a Gaussian mixture, that's k-means.
- Minimizing F over q gives **soft EM**.

## The floor you were looking for

Without bits-back, the best encoder is deterministic MAP. Its cost is:

$$
\min_z \big[-\ln p_\theta(x,z)\big] = -\ln p_\theta(x) - \ln \max_z p_\theta(z \mid x)
$$

So the overhead above the MDL is set by the **posterior**, not by $H(p(z))$:

- For a given x, the overhead is $-\ln \max_z p(z \mid x)$, the min-entropy of the posterior. It is at most $H(p(z \mid x))$.
- Averaged over the data, it is roughly the conditional entropy $H(Z \mid X)$. That's how many bits z carries that x doesn't determine.

The overhead is zero only when the posterior is a point mass, meaning x determines z. Your intuition is right that the gap can't be closed without bits-back. The quantity is just $H(Z \mid X)$, not $H(Z)$.

## Why bits-back makes sense

**The redundancy picture.** A two-part code gives each x many codewords, one for each z. Their total probability is $\sum_z p(x,z) = p(x)$. Committing to a single z discards the rest and costs $-\ln p(x,\hat z)$, which is more than $-\ln p(x)$. The fix is to make *which* codeword you pick carry information. That choice is a free side channel worth about $H(q)$ bits.

**"What if I have no random bits?"** You don't need any. The bits are **the rest of your message**, which is compressed data and therefore looks random. This is how BB-ANS works:

1. The sender *decodes* z from the current bitstream, using $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ as the model. This removes about $-\ln q(z \mid x)$ bits from the stream.
2. The sender then encodes x given z, and then z under the prior. This adds $-\ln p(x \mid z) - \ln p(z)$ bits.
3. The receiver decodes z, then x, and then *re-encodes* z with q. This recovers the borrowed bits exactly.

The net cost is $-\ln p(x,z) + \ln q(z \mid x)$, which averages to F. If there truly is nothing else to send, for example a single isolated message, the first message pays the overhead. That's the known "initial bits" cost in BB-ANS. So your skepticism is justified for one-shot coding. Bits-back is an amortized result.

**For continuous z it isn't optional.** $-\ln p(z)$ is a density, not a code length. To transmit z you have to quantize it to some precision δ, which costs about $-\ln(p(z)\,\delta)$ per dimension. That cost goes to infinity as δ → 0, so a deterministic encoder has no finite cost. Bits-back returns about $-\ln(q(z \mid x)\,\delta)$, so δ cancels and the finite F is left. Without the $H(q)$ term, the description-length reading of a continuous-latent VAE falls apart.

## Corrected summary

- **VE** is the cost without bits-back. Minimizing it gives a deterministic MAP encoder (hard EM), with an overhead of about $H(Z \mid X)$ above the MDL.
- **F**, the negative ELBO, is the cost with bits-back. Minimizing it gives the stochastic posterior encoder (soft EM), and it reaches the MDL $-\ln p_\theta(x)$.
- The difference between them is exactly $H(q)$: the bits carried for free by the choice of which codeword to use.
